## The scene

When Grace finally understands why Rocky's crewmates died, a deeper question follows: how could a species clever enough to cross between stars never have discovered radiation? The answer lies in their home. Erid's crushing atmosphere is one shield (Chapter 13.4). The other is invisible. Erid spins fast, has a large molten iron core, and, in the novel, a magnetic field at least 25 times as strong as Earth's. Together they keep every scrap of radiation from space off the ground. Eridians evolved, built a civilization and launched a starship without ever meeting it.

Earth has a magnetic shield too, weaker but real. This chapter explains how a magnetic field can push a speeding particle sideways without slowing it, and how a planet's field turns a stream of cosmic rays away.

## The concept

### Magnets and field lines

A compass needle is a small magnet that turns to line up with Earth's magnetism. Every magnet has two ends, or poles, called north and south. Unlike poles attract, like poles repel, and there is never one without the other: cut a bar magnet in half and you get two smaller magnets, each with both poles.

As with electric forces (Chapter 7.2), physicists describe this with a field. The <Term id="magnetic-field">magnetic field</Term> $\mathbf{B}$ is a vector at every point in space: its direction is the way a compass needle would point there, and its strength is measured in <Term id="tesla">teslas</Term> (T). Drawn as field lines, it runs out of a magnet's north pole and back into its south pole, crowding together where the field is strong.

| Source | Field |
|---|---|
| Earth's field at the equator | 31 µT ($3.1 \times 10^{-5}$ T) |
| Earth's field at 50° latitude | 58 µT |
| Refrigerator magnet | 1 to 10 mT |
| Sunspot | 0.15 T |
| Hospital MRI scanner | 1.5 to 7 T |
| Ordinary neutron star | about $10^{5}$ T and up |

### The force on a moving charge

A magnetic field does nothing to a charge at rest. But a charge $q$ moving at velocity $v$ through a field $B$ feels a force

$$
F = qvB\sin\theta,
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. Chapter 7.4 used this force to show why light pushes on what it hits. It has three strange features:

1. It is zero if the charge moves along the field ($\theta = 0$), and largest when it moves across it.
2. It points at right angles to *both* the velocity and the field. For a positive charge, point the fingers of your right hand along $\mathbf{v}$, curl them towards $\mathbf{B}$, and your thumb gives the direction of the force. A negative charge is pushed the opposite way.
3. Because it is always at right angles to the motion, it does no work (Chapter 5.1): it can change the direction of a charge's motion but never its speed or kinetic energy.

The formula also defines the tesla: a field of 1 T pushes with 1 N on a charge of 1 C moving across it at 1 m/s.

### Circles and spirals

A force of fixed size that always points at right angles to the motion is exactly what makes uniform circular motion (Chapter 3.2). So a charged particle moving across a uniform field goes round in a circle, whose radius is fixed by $qvB = mv^{2}/r$:

$$
r = \frac{mv}{qB}.
$$

This is the <Term id="gyroradius">gyroradius</Term>. Fast, heavy particles make big circles; strong fields make small ones. The time for one lap, $2\pi r/v = 2\pi m/(qB)$, doesn't depend on the speed at all: a faster particle goes round a bigger circle in the same time. Its frequency $f = qB/(2\pi m)$ is the <Term id="cyclotron-frequency">cyclotron frequency</Term>, after the cyclotron, a particle accelerator that gives particles a push once per lap.

If the particle also moves along the field, that part of its motion is untouched. The result is a spiral, a helix wound around a field line. Charged particles in space are tied to field lines like beads on a wire.

### Fast particles and rigidity

Cosmic rays move at nearly the speed of light. The circle argument still works with momentum instead of $mv$: the magnetic force changes only the direction of the momentum, so (Formulas) the gyroradius becomes

$$
r = \frac{p}{qB},
$$

with $p = \gamma mv$ (Chapter 10.3). What matters for bending is momentum per charge. Physicists package it as the <Term id="rigidity">rigidity</Term> $\mathcal{R} = pc/q$, which has units of volts, and quote it in gigavolts (GV). A proton with $pc = 1$ GeV has a rigidity of 1 GV, and in a field $B$ its gyroradius is $r = \mathcal{R}/(cB)$. In Earth's 31 µT that is 108 km per GV: a 10 GeV cosmic-ray proton (rigidity 10.9 GV) would circle with a radius of about 1200 km, a fifth of Earth's radius.

### Where magnetic fields come from

In 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted noticed that a wire carrying an electric current made a nearby compass needle swing. Moving charges make magnetic fields; that is their only source. An <Term id="electric-current">electric current</Term> $I$ is the charge flowing past a point per second, measured in <Term id="ampere">amperes</Term>: 1 A is 1 coulomb per second, about $6.2 \times 10^{18}$ electrons a second. (This is the SI base unit promised in Chapter 0.2. Since 2019 it is defined by fixing the electron's charge, $e = 1.602\,176\,634 \times 10^{-19}$ C.) Around a long straight wire the field lines are circles, and the field at distance $r$ is measured to be

$$
B = \frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi r},
$$

where $\mu_{0} = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6}$ N/A² is the <Term id="magnetic-constant">magnetic constant</Term>. A 100 A current gives 2 mT at 1 cm: a hundred times Earth's field. Wind the wire into a coil and you have an electromagnet. Permanent magnets work because their electrons behave like tiny current loops, all lined up.

Planets make their fields the same way, with currents. In Earth's outer core, molten iron and nickel, stirred by heat escaping from the centre and twisted by Earth's spin, carries electric currents that sustain the field: a <Term id="dynamo">dynamo</Term>. Mars once had one too, but it died about four billion years ago, leaving only patches of magnetised rock. Erid, as the novel describes it, has everything a strong dynamo needs: a big molten iron core and a day only 5.1 hours long.

### Earth's dipole

Seen from outside, Earth's field looks roughly like that of a bar magnet near its centre, a <Term id="magnetic-dipole">dipole</Term>. Far from a dipole the field weakens as $1/r^{3}$, faster than gravity's $1/r^{2}$; in the plane of the equator,

$$
B = B_{0}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{3},
$$

with $B_{0} = 31$ µT for Earth. Two Earth radii out, the field is already eight times weaker.

### Turning cosmic rays away

How strong a shield is Earth's field? A rough guess: a cosmic ray is turned back if its circle in the field is smaller than the planet, $\mathcal{R}/(cB_{0}) \lesssim R$, so the field stops rigidities below about $cB_{0}R$. For Earth, $cB_{0}R = 59$ GV. The field weakens with distance, so the real limit is lower. The Norwegian mathematician Carl Størmer worked out the exact paths in a dipole field: for a particle arriving straight down, the least rigidity that can reach the ground, the <Term id="cutoff-rigidity">cutoff rigidity</Term>, is

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\text{c}} = \frac{cB_{0}R}{4}\cos^{4}\lambda
$$

at magnetic latitude $\lambda$. At Earth's magnetic equator that is 14.8 GV: protons need at least 13.9 GeV of kinetic energy to get through. At 45° the cutoff falls to 3.7 GV, at 60° to 0.9 GV, and at the poles to zero, because there the field lines point straight down and a particle spiralling along them meets no sideways push. This is why cosmic-ray doses are higher at high latitudes and on polar flights. It is also why the auroras glow near the poles: charged particles from the Sun spiral down the field lines into the upper air and make it shine.

Erid's field, in the novel, is at least 25 times Earth's, on a planet twice Earth's size. The cutoff grows as $B_{0}R$, so Erid's is at least 50 times Earth's: about 740 GV at its equator (Example 4). Only the rarest, most energetic cosmic rays could get through, and then they would meet 15 000 g/cm² of air (Chapter 13.4).

### The magnetosphere

The Sun blows out a thin wind of protons and electrons, 3 to 10 particles per cubic centimetre at Earth's distance, moving at 250 to 750 km/s. This <Term id="solar-wind">solar wind</Term> carries its own magnetic field, and it can't easily cross Earth's field lines. Where does it stop?

A magnetic field pushes outward like a gas, with a <Term id="magnetic-pressure">magnetic pressure</Term> $B^{2}/(2\mu_{0})$ (in pascals for $B$ in teslas). It comes from the energy stored in the field, $B^{2}/(2\mu_{0})$ joules per cubic metre, just as a gas's pressure comes from its energy per volume (Chapter 11.3). The wind brings momentum: each second, a column of wind $v$ long hits each square metre and is stopped or turned aside, delivering a pressure of about $\rho v^{2}$ (Chapter 5.5). The boundary, the magnetopause, sits where the two pressures balance. With the field compressed to about twice its dipole value at the boundary, this gives about 10 Earth radii on the side facing the Sun (Formulas). The region inside, the <Term id="magnetosphere">magnetosphere</Term>, is stretched into a long tail on the night side.

Inside the magnetosphere, particles caught on field lines spiral back and forth between the hemispheres, forming the <Term id="van-allen-belts">Van Allen belts</Term>, described by James Van Allen in 1958 from the readings of the first US satellites. They span roughly 640 to 58 000 km above Earth; spacecraft pass through them quickly.

A planet without a magnetosphere is exposed. On Mars the solar wind meets the upper atmosphere directly and, over billions of years, has stripped much of it away (Chapter 14.2).

### Could a ship carry a magnetic shield?

Chapter 13.4 found that a ship coasting at 0.909c meets the interstellar gas as a beam of 1.31 GeV protons, deadly within seconds. Could a magnet deflect them instead of metres of water? Each proton has $pc = \gamma\beta m_{\text{p}}c^{2} = 2.05$ GeV. To bend it through a circle of radius 2 m, roughly a ship's width, needs $B = \mathcal{R}/(cr) = 3.4$ T, a strong MRI magnet (Example 5). At 0.995c it is 16 T. Superconducting magnets can make such fields, and studies of "active shielding" take the idea seriously, but the field must fill a large volume around the crew, and its stored energy, $B^{2}/(2\mu_{0}) = 4.6$ MJ per cubic metre at 3.4 T, has to be contained by a heavy structure. The novel's *Hail Mary* uses its Astrophage hull instead.

## Formulas

### Magnetic force and the tesla

$$
\boxed{F = qvB\sin\theta} \qquad 1\ \text{T} = 1\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{C}\cdot\text{m/s}} = 1\ \text{N/(A·m)}
$$

Direction: at right angles to $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ (right-hand rule for positive $q$). Since $\mathbf{F}$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$, the power $Fv\cos 90° = 0$: no work, constant speed.

### Circular motion in a uniform field

The force supplies the centripetal force (Chapter 3.2), with $\theta = 90°$:

$$
qvB = \frac{mv^{2}}{r} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \boxed{r = \frac{mv}{qB}} \qquad T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi m}{qB} \qquad \boxed{f = \frac{qB}{2\pi m}}
$$

### Relativistic gyroradius and rigidity

Newton's second law in the form $\mathbf{F} = d\mathbf{p}/dt$ (Chapter 5.5) holds at all speeds. The magnetic force is at right angles to $\mathbf{p}$, so it turns $\mathbf{p}$ without changing its size, just as a centripetal force turns $\mathbf{v}$. A vector of fixed size $p$ turning at angular speed $\omega$ changes at the rate $p\omega$ (Chapter 3.2), and $\omega = v/r$:

$$
\left|\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt}\right| = p\,\frac{v}{r} = qvB \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \boxed{r = \frac{p}{qB} = \frac{\mathcal{R}}{cB}} \qquad \boxed{\mathcal{R} = \frac{pc}{q}}
$$

For a proton ($q = e$), the rigidity in volts equals $pc$ in electronvolts. With $E = K + mc^{2}$ and $E^{2} = (pc)^{2} + (mc^{2})^{2}$ (Chapter 10.3), $pc = \sqrt{(K + mc^{2})^{2} - (mc^{2})^{2}}$.

### Fields from currents and from a dipole

$$
\boxed{I = \frac{dQ}{dt}} \qquad \boxed{B_{\text{wire}} = \frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi r}} \qquad \boxed{B = B_{0}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{3}\ \text{(dipole, equator)}} \qquad \mu_{0} = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{N/A}^{2}
$$

The wire formula and the dipole's $1/r^{3}$ are measured laws that full electromagnetism derives. A dipole's strength is described by its magnetic moment $m_{\text{d}} = 4\pi B_{0}R^{3}/\mu_{0}$, in A·m².

### Cutoff rigidity

The rough estimate, gyroradius equal to the planet's radius in the surface field, gives $\mathcal{R} \approx cB_{0}R$. Størmer's exact solution for particles arriving vertically, which the simulation below reproduces in the equatorial plane, is a quarter of that, falling towards the poles:

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{R}_{\text{c}} = \frac{cB_{0}R}{4}\cos^{4}\lambda}
$$

### Pressure balance at the magnetopause

The wind's dynamic pressure is $\rho v^{2}$: a slab of wind $v\,\Delta t$ thick and area $A$ has mass $\rho Av\,\Delta t$ and momentum $\rho Av^{2}\Delta t$; stopping it in time $\Delta t$ takes force $\rho Av^{2}$. The field just inside the boundary is about twice the dipole value, $2B$, giving magnetic pressure $(2B)^{2}/(2\mu_{0})$. Setting them equal with $B = B_{0}(R/r)^{3}$:

$$
\rho v^{2} = \frac{4B_{0}^{2}}{2\mu_{0}}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{6} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \boxed{\frac{r}{R} = \left(\frac{2B_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{0}\rho v^{2}}\right)^{1/6}}
$$

The sixth root makes the answer insensitive to the wind: doubling its pressure moves the boundary in by only 11%.

$$
f = \frac{eB}{2\pi m_{e}} = \frac{1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 3.1 \times 10^{-5}}{2\pi \times 9.109 \times 10^{-31}}\ \text{Hz} = 8.7 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Hz}
$$

$$
r_{\text{p}} = \frac{1.673 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg} \times 4.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m/s}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C} \times 3.1 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{T}} = 135\ \text{m}

Tiny circles compared with the planet: low-energy particles are firmly tied to the field lines.
</Example>

<Example n={2} title="A cosmic-ray proton">
Find the rigidity and the gyroradius in a 31 µT field of protons with kinetic energies of 1 GeV and 10 GeV ($m_{\text{p}}c^{2} = 938.3$ MeV).

$$
pc = \sqrt{(1000 + 938.3)^{2} - 938.3^{2}}\ \text{MeV} = 1696\ \text{MeV} \qquad \mathcal{R} = 1.70\ \text{GV}
$$

$$
r = \frac{\mathcal{R}}{cB} = \frac{1.696 \times 10^{9}\ \text{V}}{2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} \times 3.1 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{T}} = 1.82 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m} = 182\ \text{km}
$$

$$
pc = \sqrt{(10\,000 + 938.3)^{2} - 938.3^{2}}\ \text{MeV} = 10\,898\ \text{MeV} \qquad r = 182\ \text{km} \times \frac{10\,898}{1696} = 1170\ \text{km}
$$

(V/(m/s · T) is metres: 1 T = 1 V·s/m².) Comparable to Earth's radius: the field can turn such particles, but only over thousands of kilometres.
</Example>

<Example n={3} title="Earth's cutoff">
With $B_{0} = 31$ µT and $R = 6371$ km, find $cB_{0}R$, the vertical cutoff at the magnetic equator, at 45° and at 60°, and the matching proton kinetic energies.

$$
cB_{0}R = 2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} \times 3.1 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{T} \times 6.371 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m} = 5.92 \times 10^{10}\ \text{V} = 59.2\ \text{GV}
$$

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\text{c}}(0°) = \frac{59.2}{4}\ \text{GV} = 14.8\ \text{GV} \qquad \mathcal{R}_{\text{c}}(45°) = 14.8 \times 0.25 = 3.7\ \text{GV} \qquad \mathcal{R}_{\text{c}}(60°) = 14.8 \times 0.0625 = 0.93\ \text{GV}
$$

Kinetic energies $K = \sqrt{(pc)^{2} + (m_{\text{p}}c^{2})^{2}} - m_{\text{p}}c^{2}$:

$$
K(14.8\ \text{GV}) = \sqrt{14\,802^{2} + 938.3^{2}} - 938.3\ \text{MeV} = 13.9\ \text{GeV} \qquad K(3.7) = 2.88\ \text{GeV} \qquad K(0.93) = 0.38\ \text{GeV}
$$
</Example>

<Example n={4} title="Erid's shield" book>
In the novel, Erid's field is at least 25 times Earth's and its radius about twice Earth's. Find its equatorial cutoff. Then estimate where its magnetopause would be, assuming 40 Eridani A's wind at Erid's orbit (0.2 au) pushes 25 times as hard as the Sun's does at Earth, as it would if the wind thinned out as $1/d^{2}$ with the same speed.

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\text{c}} = \frac{c(25B_{0})(2R)}{4} = 50 \times 14.8\ \text{GV} = 740\ \text{GV}
$$

A proton would need about 740 GeV. Magnetopause: $(r/R)^{6} \propto B_{0}^{2}/(\rho v^{2})$, so with 25 times the field and 25 times the wind pressure,

$$
\frac{r_{\text{Erid}}}{R_{\text{Erid}}} = \frac{r_{\text{Earth}}}{R_{\text{Earth}}} \times \left(\frac{25^{2}}{25}\right)^{1/6} = 9.9 \times 25^{1/6} = 9.9 \times 1.71 = 17
$$

Erid's magnetic bubble would reach about 17 Erid radii, 34 Earth radii, towards its star.
</Example>

<Example n={5} title="A magnetic bumper for the Hail Mary" book>
At 0.909c ($\gamma = 2.400$), what field would bend interstellar protons into a circle of radius 2.0 m? Repeat for 0.9952c ($\gamma = 10.22$). What is the field's energy per cubic metre in the first case?

$$
pc = \gamma\beta\,m_{\text{p}}c^{2} = 2.400 \times 0.909 \times 938.3\ \text{MeV} = 2047\ \text{MeV} \qquad B = \frac{\mathcal{R}}{cr} = \frac{2.047 \times 10^{9}\ \text{V}}{2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} \times 2.0\ \text{m}} = 3.4\ \text{T}
$$

$$
pc = 10.22 \times 0.9952 \times 938.3\ \text{MeV} = 9544\ \text{MeV} \qquad B = \frac{9.544 \times 10^{9}}{2.998 \times 10^{8} \times 2.0}\ \text{T} = 15.9\ \text{T}
$$

$$
\frac{B^{2}}{2\mu_{0}} = \frac{(3.4\ \text{T})^{2}}{2 \times 1.2566 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{N/A}^{2}} = 4.6 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J/m}^{3}
$$

Fields like an MRI scanner's at 0.909c, beyond the strongest MRI magnets at 0.995c, and megajoules stored in every cubic metre.
</Example>

## Real physics vs. book physics

<RealVsBook>
**Established science.** The magnetic force $qvB$, gyration and cyclotron frequency, rigidity, Ørsted's discovery, Earth's dynamo and dipole field, Størmer's cutoff for a pure dipole (about 15 GV at the equator; ESA's space-environment standard fits observed cutoffs with a slightly larger constant, because the real field isn't a perfect dipole), the magnetopause at 6 to 15 Earth radii from pressure balance, the Van Allen belts and the loss of Mars's dynamo are all well established.

**Weir's inventions.** Erid's magnetic field "at least twenty-five times" Earth's, produced by its iron core, fast 5.1-hour spin and closeness to its star, is the novel's, from reader sources; so is the idea that it and the thick air keep all radiation off Erid's surface.

**How realistic are the numbers?** A strong dynamo on a big, fast-spinning world with a large iron core is plausible, though how strong a field such a planet would really have is uncertain. Closeness to the star doesn't make a planet's own field stronger; if anything, a stronger stellar wind pushes it in. With 25 times Earth's field the cutoff of about 740 GV is high enough that, with the 15 000 g/cm² atmosphere, Erid's surface would indeed be almost free of cosmic radiation. Active magnetic shields for ships are a real research idea, but the 3 to 16 tesla fields needed near light speed, filling a large volume, would be a formidable engineering problem.
</RealVsBook>

<Problem n={5} level="medium">
Explain why a magnetic field can never speed up or slow down a charged particle, using the definition of work.

<Solution>
Work is $W = Fd\cos\theta$, with $\theta$ the angle between force and displacement. The magnetic force is always at 90° to the velocity, so to every small displacement; $\cos 90° = 0$, so it does no work, and by the work–energy theorem the kinetic energy, and hence the speed, can't change.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={6} level="medium">
An aurora is made by electrons of about 10 keV spiralling down field lines where $B = 58$ µT. Find their speed (they are only mildly relativistic, so use $K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}$) and gyroradius if they move across the field.

<Solution>
$v = \sqrt{2 \times 10^{4} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}/9.109 \times 10^{-31}} = 5.9 \times 10^{7}$ m/s (0.2c). $r = 9.109 \times 10^{-31} \times 5.93 \times 10^{7}/(1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 5.8 \times 10^{-5}) = 5.8$ m.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={12} level="challenging" book>
A 3.4 T field fills a sphere of radius 3.0 m around the *Hail Mary*'s crew. How much energy is stored in it? How much mass is that, by $E = mc^{2}$?

<Solution>
Volume $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi \times 3.0^{3} = 113$ m³. $E = 4.6 \times 10^{6} \times 113 = 5.2 \times 10^{8}$ J. $m = E/c^{2} = 5.2 \times 10^{8}/8.99 \times 10^{16} = 5.8 \times 10^{-9}$ kg: about 6 micrograms. ## Summary

- A magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$, measured in teslas, pushes on moving charges with $F = qvB\sin\theta$, at right angles to both $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. It does no work, so it changes direction, never speed.
- Across a uniform field a charge circles with gyroradius $r = mv/qB$ (relativistically $p/qB$) at the cyclotron frequency $qB/2\pi m$; along the field it moves freely, so it spirals around field lines.
- Bending depends on rigidity, $\mathcal{R} = pc/q$, in volts: $r = \mathcal{R}/cB$.
- Currents make magnetic fields: $B = \mu_{0}I/2\pi r$ near a wire, with the ampere = 1 C/s. Planets' fields come from dynamos in molten iron cores. A dipole's field falls as $1/r^{3}$.
- Earth's field turns away cosmic rays below a cutoff rigidity $\mathcal{R}_{\text{c}} = (cB_{0}R/4)\cos^{4}\lambda$: 14.8 GV at the equator, none at the poles, where auroras glow.
- The solar wind is held off at the magnetopause, about 10 Earth radii out, where $\rho v^{2}$ balances the magnetic pressure $B^{2}/2\mu_{0}$.
- Erid's field, 25 times Earth's on a world twice the size, would raise the cutoff to about 740 GV. A magnetic shield for a ship at 0.909c would need about 3.4 T.

**Formulas from this chapter**

$$
F = qvB\sin\theta \qquad r = \frac{mv}{qB} = \frac{p}{qB} = \frac{\mathcal{R}}{cB} \qquad f = \frac{qB}{2\pi m} \qquad B_{\text{wire}} = \frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi r} \qquad \mathcal{R}_{\text{c}} = \frac{cB_{0}R}{4}\cos^{4}\lambda \qquad \frac{r_{\text{mp}}}{R} = \left(\frac{2B_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{0}\rho v^{2}}\right)^{1/6}
$$

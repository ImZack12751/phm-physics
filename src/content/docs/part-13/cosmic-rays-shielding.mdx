## The scene

Grace has worked out what killed Rocky's crew (Chapter 13.3): radiation. Now the puzzle is why a spacefaring species had never heard of it. Erid, Rocky explains, has a crushing, dense atmosphere, and Grace concludes that it must also have a strong magnetic field. Between them they would have shielded the Eridians from the radiation of space so completely that they never knew it existed. The *Blip-A* carried no shielding, and its crew paid for it. Only Rocky, working beside the fuel tanks full of Astrophage, was protected.

The *Hail Mary* was designed by people who knew better. In the novel, its hull has a thin layer of Astrophage slurry built in as a radiation shield, and Grace thinks hard about what the ship's nose will meet at its top speed: space is not quite empty, and at nearly the speed of light even a little hydrogen becomes a weapon.

This chapter is about the radiation of space, mostly fast atomic nuclei called cosmic rays, and about how to keep it out.

## The concept

### Rays from space

In 1912 Victor Hess took an electrometer up in a balloon. Air conducts electricity very slightly because something keeps ionizing it, and everyone assumed the cause was radioactivity in the ground, which should fade with height. Instead, at 5300 m, the ionization was twice as strong as on the ground. Something was coming in from above. These <Term id="cosmic-ray">cosmic rays</Term> won Hess the 1936 Nobel Prize.

They are not rays but particles: about 99% bare atomic nuclei and 1% electrons. Of the nuclei, about 90% are protons, 9% helium nuclei and 1% heavier nuclei, stripped of their electrons and moving at nearly the speed of light. Their energies are typically hundreds of MeV to many GeV, far more than anything from radioactive decay, and the rarest reach $3 \times 10^{20}$ eV, about 50 joules in a single nucleus. Most are thought to be accelerated by the shock waves of exploding stars, and they fill the galaxy.

Above the atmosphere, about one cosmic ray with more than 1 GeV crosses each square centimetre every second. That is what drives the 1.84 mSv per day Curiosity measured on its way to Mars (Chapter 13.3).

### Shielding is measured in mass per area

What decides how well a layer of material shields you? Chapter 13.3 found that for gamma rays it is mainly the mass of material per unit area in the way. The same is true for cosmic rays. This <Term id="column-density">column density</Term> is written $\Sigma$ and usually given in g/cm²:

$$
\Sigma = \rho x.
$$

A metre of water is 100 g/cm²; a centimetre of lead 11.35 g/cm².

A fast proton crossing matter loses about 2 MeV per g/cm² in ionization (the minimum, for particles near the speed of light), so a 1 GeV proton could in principle cross hundreds of g/cm². But long before that it usually hits a nucleus head-on and smashes it. The average column density a fast proton crosses before such a collision is the <Term id="interaction-length">nuclear interaction length</Term> $\lambda_{\text{int}}$: 83 g/cm² of water, 90 of air, 107 of aluminium, 200 of lead. The fraction of fast protons getting through a column $\Sigma$ untouched falls exponentially, just as photons do:

$$
\frac{N}{N_{0}} = e^{-\Sigma/\lambda_{\text{int}}}.
$$

But a collision doesn't end the story. The smashed nucleus sprays out a shower of secondary particles, many of them also fast and ionizing. That is why a thin shield can make things worse: it converts one cosmic ray into several.

### Earth's air is a 10-metre water shield

Our atmosphere is the best shield we have. Chapter 12.1 showed that air pressure is the weight of the air above per square metre, $P = mg/A$. So the column density of the whole atmosphere is just

$$
\Sigma = \frac{P}{g} = \frac{101\,325\ \text{Pa}}{9.807\ \text{m/s}^{2}} = 10\,330\ \text{kg/m}^{2} = 1033\ \text{g/cm}^{2},
$$

the same as a layer of water 10.3 m deep, and 11.5 interaction lengths. Only one cosmic-ray proton in about 100 000 reaches the ground without hitting a nucleus.

The ones that hit a nucleus, typically 15 to 20 km up, start an <Term id="air-shower">air shower</Term>: a cascade of short-lived particles (pions), photons, electrons and <Term id="muon">muons</Term>, heavy cousins of the electron. Muons live only 2.2 microseconds, but they move so close to the speed of light that time dilation (Chapter 10.2) lets most of them reach the ground. About one muon crosses each square centimetre at sea level every minute, and they give most of the 0.39 mSv a year that cosmic rays add to our background. The showers' neutrons also make the carbon-14 used in radiocarbon dating (Chapter 13.2).

Earth has a second shield. Cosmic rays are charged, and Earth's magnetic field bends charged particles (Part 14 covers magnetic forces), turning many of the lower-energy ones away before they reach the atmosphere. The protection is strongest near the equator and weakest near the poles. Further out, the Sun's magnetised wind sweeps away about 90% of the galaxy's cosmic rays below about 1 GeV before they reach Earth's orbit.

### Erid's sky

Erid's air is 29 atmospheres of ammonia, at a gravity of about 2 g (Chapter 12.1). Its column density is

$$
\Sigma = \frac{29 \times 101\,325\ \text{Pa}}{2.0 \times 9.81\ \text{m/s}^{2}} = 1.50 \times 10^{5}\ \text{kg/m}^{2} = 15\,000\ \text{g/cm}^{2},
$$

14.5 times Earth's: like living under 150 m of water, or 166 interaction lengths. Showers starting at the top of that atmosphere die out long before they reach the ground; even muons, which lose about 2 MeV per g/cm², would need some 30 GeV to get through. Add the strong magnetic field the novel gives Erid, and its surface would be a quieter place than any on Earth. A species living there really could have built a technology without ever noticing radiation from the sky.

### Shielding in space

Away from a planet there is no air and, far from Earth, no magnetic field. Mars's thin air, 17 g/cm², barely helps, and the dose on its surface, 0.64 mSv per day, is about a third of the dose in space, mostly because the planet itself blocks half the sky.

Shielding a spacecraft is hard. A thin aluminium wall stops the low-energy particles from solar storms, but it turns high-energy cosmic rays into showers of secondaries. Stopping them properly needs many interaction lengths, hundreds of tonnes for a crew compartment. Per kilogram, light, hydrogen-rich materials work best, because they make fewer secondaries: water, plastic, food, or fuel. Mission designers often plan to put the water tanks around the crew. Rocky's survival beside the Astrophage tanks is exactly this kind of shielding.

### Running into the interstellar gas

Interstellar space isn't empty. Near the Sun, the <Term id="interstellar-medium">interstellar medium</Term> holds about 0.3 hydrogen atoms per cubic centimetre: a very good vacuum by Earth's standards, but not nothing. For a ship crawling between planets it doesn't matter. For a ship at nearly the speed of light it matters enormously, because in the ship's frame that gas is a beam of protons, all coming head-on at the same speed, each with kinetic energy $(\gamma - 1)m_{\text{p}}c^{2}$ (Chapter 10.3).

How many hit each square metre of the ship's nose per second? In the gas's own frame there are $n$ atoms per cubic metre. In the ship's frame, distances along the motion are contracted by $\gamma$ (Chapter 10.4), so the same atoms are packed into a smaller length: the density is $\gamma n$. They arrive at speed $v$, so the flux is

$$
\Phi = \gamma n v.
$$

At 0.909c, the *Hail Mary*'s coasting speed if it used the novel's fuel (Chapter 10.6), $\gamma = 2.40$. Each proton then carries 1.31 GeV, and about $2 \times 10^{10}$ of them hit every square centimetre every second (Example 4). That is twenty billion times the cosmic-ray flux. An unshielded person at the front would receive about 6 grays per second, a lethal dose in a couple of seconds. The beam also carries power: about 41 kW per square metre, thirty times the sunlight at Earth, which the nose has to absorb and get rid of.

The beam comes from one direction, so only the nose needs serious protection, but it needs a lot. Cutting the primary protons by a factor of $e^{10} \approx 22\,000$ takes 10 interaction lengths, 8.3 m of water. In the novel, the *Hail Mary*'s hull carries a 1 mm layer of Astrophage slurry instead. With the density of water, that is 0.1 g/cm², a thousandth of an interaction length: ordinary matter that thin would do almost nothing. It works in the novel only because Astrophage absorbs everything that hits it, a property the novel calls <Term id="super-cross-sectionality">super cross-sectionality</Term> (Chapter 13.5). That is fiction, but it is consistent fiction: Astrophage thrives on the surface of stars, which are battered by fast particles all the time.

## Formulas

### Column density

$$
\boxed{\Sigma = \rho x} \qquad\text{and for a whole atmosphere}\qquad \boxed{\Sigma = \frac{P}{g}}
$$

The second follows because the pressure at the bottom of an atmosphere is the weight of the air above per unit area: $P = mg/A$, and $\Sigma = m/A$ (Chapter 12.1).

### Survival of fast protons

Each g/cm² gives each proton the same chance $1/\lambda_{\text{int}}$ of a head-on nuclear collision, so, as for gamma rays in Chapter 13.3,

$$
\frac{dN}{d\Sigma} = -\frac{N}{\lambda_{\text{int}}} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \boxed{N = N_{0}e^{-\Sigma/\lambda_{\text{int}}}}
$$

This counts only primaries that get through untouched. The secondaries they create are extra.

### The interstellar beam

**Flux.** In a time $dt$ of ship time, the nose sweeps through the gas a distance $v\,dt$. Per square metre it meets the atoms in a volume $v\,dt$, at the contracted density $\gamma n$:

$$
\boxed{\Phi = \gamma n v}
$$

(Seen from the gas, the ship sweeps $n v\,dt_{\text{gas}}$ atoms per square metre in a gas time $dt_{\text{gas}} = \gamma\,dt$ (Chapter 10.2): the same answer.)

**Energy per proton.** $K = (\gamma - 1)m_{\text{p}}c^{2}$, with $m_{\text{p}}c^{2} = 938.3$ MeV.

**Power per square metre of nose.**

$$
\boxed{\frac{P}{A} = \Phi K}
$$

**Dose rate.** Each proton crossing tissue deposits about $dE/d\Sigma \approx 2$ MeV per g/cm² (the minimum for fast particles, so this is a lower limit). A flux $\Phi$ (per cm² per second) through a layer of $\Sigma$ g/cm² deposits $\Phi\,(dE/d\Sigma)\,\Sigma$ per cm² each second in a mass $\Sigma$ per cm², so the dose rate is

$$
\boxed{\dot{D} = \Phi\,\frac{dE}{d\Sigma}}
$$

in MeV per gram per second, which converts to Gy/s with $1\ \text{MeV/g} = 1.602 \times 10^{-10}$ J/kg.

## Worked examples

<Example n={1} title="Earth's air as a shield">
Find the column density of Earth's atmosphere, its equivalent depth of water, the number of interaction lengths ($\lambda_{\text{int}} = 90.1$ g/cm² for air), and the fraction of cosmic-ray protons that reach the ground without a nuclear collision.

$$
\Sigma = \frac{101\,325\ \text{Pa}}{9.807\ \text{m/s}^{2}} = 1.033 \times 10^{4}\ \text{kg/m}^{2} = 1033\ \text{g/cm}^{2}
$$

Water of density $1000\ \text{kg/m}^{3}$ with the same column is $10\,330/1000 = 10.3$ m deep.

$$
\frac{\Sigma}{\lambda_{\text{int}}} = \frac{1033}{90.1} = 11.5 \qquad \frac{N}{N_{0}} = e^{-11.5} = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}
$$

One in 100 000. The cosmic radiation we get at the ground is almost all secondaries.
</Example>

<Example n={2} title="Erid's sky" book>
Erid's air is at 29 atm with surface gravity about 2.0 g. Find its column density, its water equivalent and its number of interaction lengths (taking 90 g/cm², as for air). Compare with Earth.

$$
\Sigma = \frac{29 \times 101\,325\ \text{Pa}}{2.0 \times 9.81\ \text{m/s}^{2}} = 1.50 \times 10^{5}\ \text{kg/m}^{2} = 1.50 \times 10^{4}\ \text{g/cm}^{2}
$$

$$
\text{water: } \frac{1.50 \times 10^{5}}{1000} = 150\ \text{m} \qquad \frac{\Sigma}{\lambda_{\text{int}}} = \frac{15\,000}{90.1} = 166 \qquad \frac{15\,000}{1033} = 14.5
$$

A muon losing 2 MeV per g/cm² would need $2 \times 15\,000 = 30\,000$ MeV = 30 GeV to cross it. The Eridians live under the equivalent of 150 m of water.
</Example>

<Example n={3} title="On the surface of Mars">
Mars's surface pressure is 636 Pa and its gravity 3.73 m/s². Find the column density of its air in g/cm² and interaction lengths. Curiosity measured 0.64 mSv/day on the surface and 1.84 in space. What fraction of the space dose reaches the surface?

$$
\Sigma = \frac{636\ \text{Pa}}{3.73\ \text{m/s}^{2}} = 171\ \text{kg/m}^{2} = 17\ \text{g/cm}^{2} = 0.19\,\lambda_{\text{int}}
$$

$$
\frac{0.64}{1.84} = 0.35
$$

The air stops little. Most of the reduction, to about a half, comes from the planet blocking the whole lower half of the sky.
</Example>

<Example n={4} title="Coasting through interstellar gas" book>
With the novel's fuel, Chapter 10.6 found the *Hail Mary* would coast at $0.909c$ ($\gamma = 2.400$). Take $n = 0.3$ atoms per cm³. Find the energy of each proton in the ship's frame, the flux on the nose, the dose rate to unshielded tissue there, and the power per square metre.

$$
K = (\gamma - 1)m_{\text{p}}c^{2} = 1.400 \times 938.3\ \text{MeV} = 1314\ \text{MeV} = 1.31\ \text{GeV}
$$

$$
\Phi = \gamma n v = 2.400 \times 0.3 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}^{-3} \times 0.909 \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} = 1.96 \times 10^{14}\ \text{m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1} = 1.96 \times 10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}
$$

$$
\dot{D} = 1.96 \times 10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1} \times 1.98\ \text{MeV cm}^{2}/\text{g} = 3.9 \times 10^{10}\ \text{MeV/(g·s)} = 3.9 \times 10^{10} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{Gy/s} = 6.2\ \text{Gy/s}
$$

$$
\frac{P}{A} = 1.96 \times 10^{14}\ \text{m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1} \times 1314 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J} = 4.1 \times 10^{4}\ \text{W/m}^{2}
$$

At least 6 Gy every second (an almost-always-fatal 8 Gy in under 2 s), and 41 kW/m² of heating, 30 times the sunlight at Earth. Nothing alive can sit unshielded at the front of a ship crossing the galaxy at such speeds.
</Example>

<Example n={5} title="At the flip" book>
On the course's relativistic 1.5 g flip-and-burn (Chapter 10.6), the *Hail Mary* reaches $0.9952c$, $\gamma = 10.22$, at the flip. Repeat Example 4. Then find the mass of water needed to cut the primary protons by a factor $e^{10}$ over a 10 m² nose.

$$
K = 9.22 \times 938.3\ \text{MeV} = 8.65\ \text{GeV}
$$

$$
\Phi = 10.22 \times 0.3 \times 10^{6} \times 0.9952 \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1} = 9.15 \times 10^{14}\ \text{m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}
$$

$$
\dot{D} = 9.15 \times 10^{10} \times 1.98 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{Gy/s} = 29\ \text{Gy/s} \qquad \frac{P}{A} = 9.15 \times 10^{14} \times 8650 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{W/m}^{2} = 1.27 \times 10^{6}\ \text{W/m}^{2}
$$

Shield: $10 \times 83.3 = 833$ g/cm² of water, a layer 8.33 m thick:

$$
m = 8.33\ \text{m} \times 10\ \text{m}^{2} \times 1000\ \text{kg/m}^{3} = 8.3 \times 10^{4}\ \text{kg},
$$

about as much as the whole empty ship in this course's model (Chapter 5.6). And it would still have to shed more than a megawatt per square metre as heat. The novel's 1 mm of slurry, 0.1 g/cm², is 8000 times too little for ordinary matter.
</Example>

## Real physics vs. book physics

<RealVsBook>
**Established science.** Hess's 1912 balloon flights, the composition and energies of cosmic rays, air showers and muons, the nuclear interaction lengths (Particle Data Group), the column density of Earth's air, the magnetic and solar-wind shielding of Earth, and the difficulty of shielding spacecraft are all well established. The local interstellar gas density of about 0.3 atoms per cm³ is a measured value. The hazard of interstellar gas to relativistic ships is a standard result in studies of interstellar travel.

**Weir's inventions.** Erid's strong magnetic field, the *Blip-A*'s lack of shielding, Rocky's survival beside the Astrophage tanks, and the *Hail Mary*'s 1 mm Astrophage slurry layer, which absorbs all incoming particles, are the novel's (from reader sources). Erid's 29 atm and our 2.0 g are the course's model from Part 12.

**How realistic are the numbers?** Erid's atmosphere alone would make the Eridians' ignorance believable: 15 000 g/cm² of air is better shielding than many underground laboratories have. The *Hail Mary*'s need for a nose shield is real, and our estimate of at least 6 Gy per second at 0.909c shows it is a matter of life and death within seconds. A 1 mm layer of any ordinary substance could not do the job; the novel's super-absorbing Astrophage is the invention that makes it work. Our dose estimate counts only ionization by the primary protons; nuclear collisions and their showers would add more.
</RealVsBook>

<Problem n={7} level="medium">
A ship moves at $0.5c$ through gas with 0.3 atoms per cm³. Find $\gamma$, the proton energy, the flux and the dose rate to unshielded tissue (2 MeV cm²/g).

<Solution>
$\gamma = 1/\sqrt{0.75} = 1.155$. $K = 0.155 \times 938.3 = 145$ MeV. $\Phi = 1.155 \times 0.3 \times 10^{6} \times 1.5 \times 10^{8} = 5.2 \times 10^{13}\ \text{m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1} = 5.2 \times 10^{9}$ per cm² per s. $\dot{D} = 5.2 \times 10^{9} \times 1.98 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-10} = 1.6$ Gy/s. </Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={8} level="medium">
Why is the flux $\gamma nv$ and not just $nv$? Explain in words from the ship's point of view and from the gas's point of view.

<Solution>
From the ship: the gas is length-contracted along the motion, so its density is $\gamma n$, and it arrives at speed $v$. From the gas: the ship sweeps $nv$ atoms per m² per second of gas time, but the ship's clocks run slow, so one second of ship time is $\gamma$ seconds of gas time, and the ship counts $\gamma nv$ per second of its own time. Both views agree.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={10} level="challenging">
At what speed does an unshielded nose receive 8 Gy in one minute through gas of 0.3 atoms per cm³ (use 2 MeV cm²/g)?

</Solution>

## Summary

- Cosmic rays are fast nuclei from space: 90% protons, 9% helium, 1% heavier, with energies of hundreds of MeV to many GeV. Hess found them in 1912.
- Shielding is measured as column density, mass per area, $\Sigma = \rho x$. A fast proton travels on average one nuclear interaction length (about 83 to 90 g/cm² of water or air) before smashing a nucleus; primaries surviving fall as $e^{-\Sigma/\lambda_{\text{int}}}$, but the collisions make showers of secondaries.
- Earth's air is 1033 g/cm², 10 m of water, 11.5 interaction lengths; at the ground we mostly get muons. Earth's magnetic field and the Sun's wind deflect many cosmic rays before they arrive.
- Erid's 29 atm at 2 g is 15 000 g/cm², 150 m of water and 166 interaction lengths. With its magnetic field, the Eridians could easily have never met cosmic radiation.
- In deep space the dose is about 1.84 mSv per day, and shielding against it takes hundreds of tonnes; hydrogen-rich materials such as water, food and fuel work best. Rocky's fuel tanks shielded him.
- A ship near light speed meets interstellar gas as a beam: flux $\gamma nv$, each proton $(\gamma - 1)m_{\text{p}}c^{2}$. At 0.909c that is at least 6 Gy per second and 41 kW/m² at an unshielded nose. Ordinary matter would need metres of shielding; the *Hail Mary*'s 1 mm slurry works only because Astrophage absorbs everything.

**Formulas from this chapter**

$$
\Sigma = \rho x = \frac{P}{g} \qquad N = N_{0}e^{-\Sigma/\lambda_{\text{int}}} \qquad \Phi = \gamma n v \qquad K = (\gamma - 1)m_{\text{p}}c^{2} \qquad \frac{P}{A} = \Phi K \qquad \dot{D} = \Phi\,\frac{dE}{d\Sigma}
$$

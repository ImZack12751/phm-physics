## The scene

Grace is a scientist with a lab, a supply of Astrophage and a lot of unanswered questions. Again and again in the novel he runs an experiment, writes down a column of numbers and tries to see the pattern in it. Back on Earth, the scientists who first spotted the Sun dimming were doing the same: plotting measurements over time and asking where the line was heading.

A table of numbers hides its pattern. A graph shows it at a glance. A graph also does more than show: its *slope* is a rate, the *area under it* is a total, and the right choice of axes can turn a curve into a straight line that reveals the law behind the data. This chapter teaches all three.

## The concept

### Anatomy of a graph

When you take measurements, you usually choose one quantity and measure how another responds. The quantity you control is the <Term id="independent-variable">independent variable</Term>. It goes along the horizontal axis (the $x$-axis). The one that responds is the <Term id="dependent-variable">dependent variable</Term>, and it goes up the vertical axis (the $y$-axis).

Label each axis with the quantity **and its unit**, as in "time, $t$ (s)". A graph without units is as useless as a number without units.

### Straight lines

Many relationships give a straight line. Every straight line has the equation

$$
y = mx + c,
$$

where:

- $m$ is the <Term id="gradient">gradient</Term> (slope): how much $y$ rises for each unit step in $x$;
- $c$ is the <Term id="intercept">intercept</Term>: the value of $y$ where the line crosses the $y$-axis (at $x = 0$).

If $c = 0$ the line passes through the origin, and $y$ is directly proportional to $x$, as in [the proportionality chapter](/part-0/ratios-proportionality/).

### The gradient is a rate, with units

The gradient divides a change in $y$ by a change in $x$, so its unit is (unit of $y$) ÷ (unit of $x$). On a distance–time graph that is m/s: **the gradient is the speed**. On a graph of fuel left against time, it is kg/s, the rate fuel is used. Whenever you see a gradient, ask "what rate is this?"

A *curved* graph has a gradient that changes from point to point. Its gradient at a single point is the gradient of the straight line that just touches the curve there. Making that precise is the first job of calculus, in Part 0.5.

### The area under a graph is a total

On a speed–time graph, the area between the line and the time axis has units of (m/s) × s = m. It is the **distance travelled**. That makes sense: at a steady 30 m/s for 6 s, you cover $30 \times 6 = 180$ m, which is exactly the area of a rectangle 6 s wide and 30 m/s tall.

Gradients and areas are opposite operations: one turns totals into rates, and the other turns rates into totals. That relationship is the heart of calculus.

### Reading between and beyond the points

Estimating a value *between* measured points is <Term id="interpolation">interpolation</Term>, and it is usually safe. Estimating *beyond* the measurements is <Term id="extrapolation">extrapolation</Term>, and it is risky, because you are assuming the pattern carries on. The novel's dimming forecast is an extrapolation. Problem 7 shows how much the answer depends on which pattern you assume.

### Real data scatter

Real measurements never fall exactly on a line, because every measurement has some uncertainty. So scientists draw a **best-fit line** that passes as close as possible to all the points, with roughly as many points above it as below. Computers find it by a method called *least squares*: they choose the line that makes the total of the squared vertical misses as small as possible. The simulation below uses it.

### Straightening curves: finding the law

Our eyes judge straight lines well but curves badly. Is a curve a $1/x$ or a $1/x^{2}$? It's hard to tell. The trick is to change what you plot so that the *expected* law becomes a straight line:

| If you suspect | Plot | You get a straight line with gradient |
|---|---|---|
| $y = kx^{2}$ | $y$ against $x^{2}$ | $k$ |
| $y = k/x^{2}$ | $y$ against $1/x^{2}$ | $k$ |
| $y = k\sqrt{x}$ | $y^{2}$ against $x$ | $k^{2}$ |
| $y = Ae^{kx}$ (exponential) | $\ln y$ against $x$ | $k$ |
| $y = kx^{n}$ (any power, $n$ unknown) | $\log y$ against $\log x$ | $n$ |

If the points fall on a straight line, your guess is confirmed, and the gradient gives you the constant. That is how a lot of physics was discovered.

## Formulas

### Gradient from two points

Between two points $(x_{1}, y_{1})$ and $(x_{2}, y_{2})$ on a straight line, the rise is $\Delta y = y_{2} - y_{1}$ and the run is $\Delta x = x_{2} - x_{1}$ ($\Delta$, the Greek capital delta, means "change in"):

$$
m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_{2} - y_{1}}{x_{2} - x_{1}}
$$

Then the intercept follows from $y_{1} = mx_{1} + c$, rearranged to $c = y_{1} - mx_{1}$.

### Why log plots straighten exponentials

Take $y = Ae^{kx}$ and take the natural log of both sides. By the product and power laws of logarithms:

$$
\ln y = \ln A + \ln(e^{kx}) = \ln A + kx
$$

Compare with $Y = mX + c$: plotting $Y = \ln y$ against $X = x$ gives a straight line with gradient $m = k$ and intercept $c = \ln A$. (With $\log_{10}$ instead, the gradient is $k\log_{10}e = 0.4343k$.)

### Why log–log plots straighten power laws

Take $y = kx^{n}$ and take logs of both sides:

$$
\log y = \log k + n\log x
$$

Plotting $\log y$ against $\log x$ gives a straight line of gradient $n$, the power, and intercept $\log k$. The base of the logarithm doesn't matter, as long as both axes use the same one.

## Worked examples

<Example n={1} title="Reading a speed from a graph">
A probe's distance from a ship is plotted against time. The straight line passes through $(2.0\ \text{s}, 50\ \text{m})$ and $(8.0\ \text{s}, 140\ \text{m})$. Find its speed and its distance at $t = 0$.

$$
m = \frac{140\ \text{m} - 50\ \text{m}}{8.0\ \text{s} - 2.0\ \text{s}} = \frac{90\ \text{m}}{6.0\ \text{s}} = 15\ \text{m/s}
$$

$$
c = y_{1} - mx_{1} = 50\ \text{m} - 15\ \text{m/s} \times 2.0\ \text{s} = 50\ \text{m} - 30\ \text{m} = 20\ \text{m}
$$

So $d = 20\ \text{m} + (15\ \text{m/s})\,t$. The probe moves at 15 m/s and started 20 m away.
</Example>

<Example n={2} title="Calibrating a home-made thermometer" book>
Suppose Grace builds a temperature sensor whose reading $R$ (in arbitrary units) changes steadily with temperature. In melting ice ($0\ °\text{C}$) it reads 12. In boiling water ($100\ °\text{C}$) it reads 87. (a) Find the formula for $T$ in terms of $R$. (b) What temperature does a reading of 62 mean? (c) What would it read for Astrophage at $96.415\ °\text{C}$?

(a) Put $T$ on the $y$-axis and $R$ on the $x$-axis. The two points are $(12, 0\ °\text{C})$ and $(87, 100\ °\text{C})$:

$$
m = \frac{100\ °\text{C} - 0\ °\text{C}}{87 - 12} = \frac{100}{75}\ °\text{C} = 1.333\ °\text{C per unit}, \qquad c = 0 - 1.333 \times 12 = -16.0\ °\text{C}
$$

So $T = 1.333R - 16.0$ (in °C), which is the same as $T = \tfrac{4}{3}(R - 12)$.

(b) $T = \tfrac{4}{3}(62 - 12) = \tfrac{4}{3} \times 50 = 66.7\ °\text{C}$. That is interpolation, so it is reliable.

(c) Rearrange: $R = \tfrac{3}{4}T + 12 = 0.75 \times 96.415 + 12 = 84.3$.

This is exactly how the Celsius scale itself was built: two fixed points and a straight line between them, as in [Handling units](/part-0/unit-handling/).
</Example>

<Example n={3} title="Distance from a speed–time graph">
A ship's speed rises steadily from 0 to 30 m/s in 10 s, then stays at 30 m/s for 6 s (the graph above). How far does it go?

Area of the triangle: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 10\ \text{s} \times 30\ \text{m/s} = 150\ \text{m}$.

Area of the rectangle: $6\ \text{s} \times 30\ \text{m/s} = 180\ \text{m}$.

Total: $150\ \text{m} + 180\ \text{m} = 330\ \text{m}$.
</Example>

<Example n={4} title="Straightening the planets' sunlight">
NASA's measured intensities of sunlight at the planets (Mercury 9082.7, Venus 2601.3, Earth 1361.0, Mars 586.2, Jupiter 50.26 and Saturn 14.82 W/m²) form a steep curve against distance. Show that plotting $I$ against $1/r^{2}$ (with $r$ in au) gives a straight line, and use its gradient to find the Sun's luminosity.

If $I = \dfrac{L}{4\pi r^{2}}$, then $I = \left(\dfrac{L}{4\pi}\right) \times \dfrac{1}{r^{2}}$. That has the form $y = mx$ with $y = I$, $x = 1/r^{2}$ and gradient $m = L/(4\pi)$. Earth's point is $x = 1/1^{2} = 1\ \text{au}^{-2}$, $y = 1361\ \text{W/m}^{2}$, and the line passes through the origin, so

$$
m = 1361\ \text{W/m}^{2} \text{ per au}^{-2} = 1361\ \text{W/m}^{2} \times \text{au}^{2}.
$$

Mars confirms it: $x = 1/1.5238^{2} = 0.4307$ and $y = 586.2$, so $y/x = 1361$ ✓. Every planet gives the same gradient, so the points form a perfect straight line through the origin.

The luminosity is then

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= 4\pi m = 4\pi \times 1361\ \text{W/m}^{2} \times (1.496 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m})^{2} \\
  &= 4\pi \times 1361 \times 2.238 \times 10^{22}\ \text{W} \\
  &= 3.83 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}.
\end{aligned}
$$

From a straight-line fit through six planets, we have recovered the Sun's total power output.
</Example>

<Example n={5} title="A log plot reveals the doubling time" book>
The dimming model from [the last chapter](/part-0/exponentials-logarithms/) predicts $D = 1.00\%$ at 9 years and $D = 5.00\%$ at 20 years. If you plot $\log_{10}D$ against $t$, what is the gradient, and what doubling time does it imply?

$$
m = \frac{\log_{10}5.00 - \log_{10}1.00}{20\ \text{yr} - 9\ \text{yr}} = \frac{0.6990 - 0}{11\ \text{yr}} = 0.0635\ \text{per year}
$$

Each year, $\log_{10}D$ rises by 0.0635. A doubling raises $\log_{10}D$ by $\log_{10}2 = 0.3010$, which takes

$$
T = \frac{0.3010}{0.0635\ \text{per year}} = 4.74\ \text{yr},
$$

matching the previous chapter. On a log plot the doubling time can be read straight off the slope.
</Example>

## Real physics vs. book physics

<RealVsBook>
**Established science.** Gradients as rates, areas as totals, best-fit lines and log plots are standard tools of experimental science. The planetary sunlight data really do fall on a perfect $1/r^{2}$ line, because NASA's values come from the inverse-square law, which satellites have confirmed directly.

**Weir's inventions.** The thermometer in Worked example 2 and its readings are our own invention, in the spirit of Grace's improvised lab work.

**How realistic is the forecasting?** Extrapolating a measured trend is exactly what real scientists do. The danger, which Problem 7 shows, is that two models that fit the same early data can disagree wildly about the future. Scientists handle this by understanding the *mechanism*, not just the curve. In the novel, knowing that Astrophage breeds is what makes an exponential model the right choice.
</RealVsBook>

## Practice set

<Problem n={1} level="easy">
For the line $y = 3x - 2$: (a) what are the gradient and intercept? (b) What is $y$ when $x = 4$? (c) Where does the line cross the $x$-axis?

<Solution>
(a) Gradient 3, intercept $-2$.

(b) $y = 3 \times 4 - 2 = 10$.

(c) On the $x$-axis, $y = 0$: $0 = 3x - 2$, so $x = 2/3 \approx 0.67$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={2} level="easy">
A graph of distance (km) against time (h) is a straight line through $(0, 5)$ and $(4, 25)$. Give the gradient with its unit and say what it means physically.

<Solution>
$m = \dfrac{25\ \text{km} - 5\ \text{km}}{4\ \text{h} - 0\ \text{h}} = \dfrac{20\ \text{km}}{4\ \text{h}} = 5\ \text{km/h}$. It is the speed: the walker covers 5 km each hour, having started 5 km from the reference point.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={3} level="easy">
An experimenter moves a light meter to different distances from a lamp and records the reading at each. Which is the independent variable and which is the dependent variable? Which goes on which axis?

<Solution>
The distance is chosen by the experimenter, so it is the independent variable and goes on the $x$-axis. The meter reading responds, so it is the dependent variable and goes on the $y$-axis.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={4} level="medium">
A rover moves at a steady $12$ m/s for 5.0 s, then slows steadily to a stop over the next 4.0 s. Sketch the speed–time graph and find the total distance.

<Solution>
The graph is a rectangle (12 m/s tall, 5.0 s wide) followed by a triangle sloping down to zero over 4.0 s.

Rectangle: $12\ \text{m/s} \times 5.0\ \text{s} = 60$ m. Triangle: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 4.0\ \text{s} \times 12\ \text{m/s} = 24$ m. Total: $84$ m.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={5} level="medium">
Find the equation of the straight line through $(2, 7)$ and $(6, 19)$.

<Solution>
$m = \dfrac{19 - 7}{6 - 2} = \dfrac{12}{4} = 3$. $c = 7 - 3 \times 2 = 1$. So $y = 3x + 1$.

Check with the other point: $3 \times 6 + 1 = 19$ ✓.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={6} level="medium">
You suspect $y = k/x$. What should you plot to get a straight line, and what would its gradient be? What would a graph of $y$ against $x$ look like?

<Solution>
Plot $y$ against $1/x$. Since $y = k \times (1/x)$, you get a straight line through the origin with gradient $k$. Plotted against $x$ itself, the graph is a curve that falls steeply at first and then levels off towards the axis, the green curve in the [proportionality chapter's figure](/part-0/ratios-proportionality/).
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={7} level="medium" book>
Two models both fit the novel's forecasts of 1% dimming at 9 years and 5% at 20 years: a straight line and an exponential (doubling time 4.74 yr). What does each predict at 30 years?

<Solution>
**Straight line** through $(9, 1)$ and $(20, 5)$: $m = \dfrac{5 - 1}{20 - 9} = \dfrac{4}{11} = 0.364\ \%$ per year. At 30 years: $D = 5 + 0.364 \times 10 = 8.6\%$.

**Exponential:** $D = 5\% \times 2^{10/4.74} = 21.6\%$ (from the previous chapter).

The two models agree at the measured points but differ by a factor of 2.5 just ten years later. Extrapolation is only as good as the model behind it.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={8} level="medium">
Two points on a power law $y = kx^{n}$ are $(2, 24)$ and $(4, 192)$. Use the log–log idea to find $n$, then find $k$.

<Solution>
The log–log gradient is

$$
n = \frac{\log 192 - \log 24}{\log 4 - \log 2} = \frac{\log(192/24)}{\log(4/2)} = \frac{\log 8}{\log 2} = 3.
$$

Then $k = \dfrac{y}{x^{3}} = \dfrac{24}{8} = 3$. So $y = 3x^{3}$. Check: $3 \times 4^{3} = 192$ ✓.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={9} level="challenging" book>
For a pendulum, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, so $T^{2} = \dfrac{4\pi^{2}}{g}L$. Plotting $T^{2}$ against $L$ gives a straight line. (a) What gradient would you expect on Earth ($g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^{2}$)? (b) Grace's plot gives a gradient of $2.69\ \text{s}^{2}/\text{m}$. What is $g$ on the *Hail Mary*?

<Solution>
(a) Gradient $= \dfrac{4\pi^{2}}{g} = \dfrac{39.48}{9.81\ \text{m/s}^{2}} = 4.02\ \text{s}^{2}/\text{m}$.

(b) Rearranging, $g = \dfrac{4\pi^{2}}{\text{gradient}} = \dfrac{39.48}{2.69\ \text{s}^{2}/\text{m}} = 14.7\ \text{m/s}^{2}$, which is 1.5 times Earth's gravity.

Using the gradient of a best-fit line, rather than a single measurement, averages out the random errors in each swing.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={10} level="challenging">
A radioactive sample's count rate falls from 800 counts per minute to 200 counts per minute in 10.0 minutes. Plotting $\ln(\text{count rate})$ against $t$ gives a straight line. Find its gradient and the half-life.

<Solution>
$$
m = \frac{\ln 200 - \ln 800}{10.0\ \text{min}} = \frac{\ln(1/4)}{10.0\ \text{min}} = \frac{-1.386}{10.0\ \text{min}} = -0.139\ \text{per min}
$$

That is $N = N_{0}e^{-0.139t}$, a decay with $k = -0.139$ per minute. The half-life is $t_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{0.139} = 5.0$ min. Check: from 800 to 200 is two halvings in 10 minutes, so 5 minutes each ✓.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={11} level="challenging">
A solar panel's power per square metre (W/m²) is plotted against time (s). What are the units of the area under this graph? What does that area represent? (Use the plain-language meaning of the watt: an amount of energy per second.)

<Solution>
Area units: $\dfrac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^{2}} \times \text{s}$. A watt is energy per second, so W × s is an amount of energy, and the area is the **energy collected per square metre** of panel. (Part 5 names this unit of energy the joule: $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{W} \times 1\ \text{s}$.) Integrating power over time, as we will call it in Part 0.5, gives energy.
</Solution>
</Problem>

## Summary

- Independent variable on $x$, dependent on $y$. Label every axis with its quantity and unit.
- A straight line is $y = mx + c$. The gradient $m = \Delta y/\Delta x$ is a rate, with units of $y$ per unit of $x$. The intercept $c$ is the value at $x = 0$.
- The area under a graph is a total, with units of $y$ times units of $x$. Under a speed–time graph, it is distance.
- Interpolating is safe. Extrapolating assumes the pattern continues.
- Change the axes to straighten a curve: $1/x^{2}$ for inverse-square, $\ln y$ for exponentials, log–log for power laws. The gradient then gives the constant or the power.

**Formulas from this chapter**

$$
y = mx + c \qquad m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_{2} - y_{1}}{x_{2} - x_{1}} \qquad c = y_{1} - mx_{1}
$$

$$
y = Ae^{kx} \Rightarrow \ln y = \ln A + kx \qquad\qquad y = kx^{n} \Rightarrow \log y = \log k + n\log x
$$

## The scene

The whole mission depends on one number: Tau Ceti is about 11.9 light-years away. Nobody has ever stretched a tape measure that far. Astronomers measured it with geometry, by watching how the star seems to shift a tiny amount against the background as Earth moves around the Sun. When Grace finally reaches Tau Ceti and looks back, the Sun is just one more bright point among the stars. How big or small something *looks* depends on angles and distances in the same way.

Turning angles into distances is the job of trigonometry. You have probably met it at school. This chapter refreshes it and adds the three things physics needs most: radians, the unit circle and small angles. All three come back often, especially for circular motion in Part 3 and for waves in Part 7.

## The concept

### Angles in degrees and radians

An angle measures an amount of turning. In everyday life we divide a full turn into 360 <Term id="degree-angle">degrees</Term>. That number is an ancient choice, convenient because 360 divides evenly in many ways, but it has nothing to do with circles themselves.

Physics prefers a natural unit, the <Term id="radian">radian</Term>. Take the radius of a circle and bend it so it lies along the circle's edge. The angle it covers at the centre is one radian.

<Figure caption="One radian is the angle whose arc is exactly one radius long. A full circle is 2π radians." alt="A circle with two radii drawn from the centre. The arc between their ends is highlighted in amber and labelled 'arc = r', and both radii are labelled r. The angle between them is marked 1 rad. Text alongside: one radian is about 57.3 degrees; the whole edge is 2 pi r long, about 6.28 radii, so a full turn is 2 pi radians, which is 360 degrees; arc length s equals r theta, with theta in radians.">
	<RadianDiagram />
</Figure>

How many radians make a full turn? The circumference is $2\pi r$, so $2\pi \approx 6.28$ radii fit around the edge. A full turn is therefore $2\pi$ radians. That gives the conversion

$$
360° = 2\pi\ \text{rad} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad 180° = \pi\ \text{rad}.
$$

Why bother? Because with radians, arc length is simply radius times angle. As you'll see, this makes many formulas in physics clean, where degrees would clutter them with factors of $\pi/180$.

### Right-angled triangles

In a right-angled triangle, name the sides relative to one of the other angles, $\theta$ (the Greek letter theta):

<Figure caption="Sides of a right-angled triangle, named from the point of view of the angle θ." alt="A right-angled triangle with the right angle at the bottom right. The angle theta is at the bottom left. The longest side, from bottom left to top right, is labelled hypotenuse. The vertical side on the right, facing theta, is labelled opposite. The horizontal bottom side next to theta is labelled adjacent.">
	<RightTriangle />
</Figure>

The key fact behind trigonometry is this. **All right-angled triangles with the same angle θ have the same shape, just scaled up or down.** Scaling multiplies every side by the same factor, so the *ratios* between sides don't change. Those ratios depend only on the angle, and they have names:

$$
\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad
\cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad
\tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}.
$$

These are the <Term id="sine">sine</Term>, <Term id="cosine">cosine</Term> and <Term id="tangent">tangent</Term>. The classic memory aid is **SOH CAH TOA**: Sine = Opposite/Hypotenuse, Cosine = Adjacent/Hypotenuse, Tangent = Opposite/Adjacent. Because they are ratios of two lengths, they have no units.

To go the other way, from a ratio to an angle, use the inverse functions $\sin^{-1}$, $\cos^{-1}$ and $\tan^{-1}$ (also written arcsin, arccos and arctan). For example, if $\tan\theta = 0.3$, then $\theta = \tan^{-1}(0.3) = 16.7°$. Be careful: $\sin^{-1}$ means "the angle whose sine is", **not** $1/\sin$.

**Calculator warning.** Every calculator has a degrees/radians mode. Getting it wrong is the commonest trig mistake there is. $\sin 30 = 0.5$ in degree mode, but $\sin(30\ \text{rad}) = -0.988$.

### Pythagoras' theorem

For any right-angled triangle with shorter sides $a$, $b$ and hypotenuse $c$:

$$
a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}
$$

It's worth seeing why. Arrange four copies of the triangle inside a square of side $a + b$:

<Figure caption="A picture proof of Pythagoras' theorem. Count the big square's area two ways and the 2ab terms cancel." alt="A square of side a plus b. Four identical blue right triangles with legs a and b sit in its corners, leaving a tilted amber square of side c in the middle, labelled c squared. Text alongside: a plus b all squared equals c squared plus 4 times half a b; expanding gives a squared plus 2ab plus b squared equals c squared plus 2ab; so a squared plus b squared equals c squared.">
	<PythagorasProof />
</Figure>

### Special angles worth knowing

| $\theta$ | $0°$ | $30°$ | $45°$ | $60°$ | $90°$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| radians | $0$ | $\pi/6$ | $\pi/4$ | $\pi/3$ | $\pi/2$ |
| $\sin\theta$ | $0$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.707$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.866$ | $1$ |
| $\cos\theta$ | $1$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $0$ |
| $\tan\theta$ | $0$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.577$ | $1$ | $\sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$ | undefined |

### The unit circle: angles beyond 90°

A triangle can't have a second angle of 120°, yet physics needs sines and cosines of any angle: a spinning ship turns through all of them. The fix is the <Term id="unit-circle">unit circle</Term>, a circle of radius 1 centred on the origin of a graph. Start at the point $(1, 0)$ and turn anticlockwise through angle $\theta$. Where you land is defined to be

$$
(x, y) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta).
$$

For angles between 0° and 90°, this agrees with SOH CAH TOA: the hypotenuse is the radius, 1, so the opposite side *is* $\sin\theta$ and the adjacent side *is* $\cos\theta$. Beyond 90° it simply keeps going. In the second quarter of the circle, $x$ is negative, so cosine is negative, while sine stays positive. Going all the way round, $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ trace out smooth, repeating waves, as the simulation below shows. That is why sines and cosines describe everything that repeats, from pendulums to sound to light.

### Small angles

Astronomy is full of tiny angles. For a small angle in **radians**, the arc and the straight vertical side of the triangle are almost the same length, and the adjacent side is almost the same as the hypotenuse. That gives the <Term id="small-angle-approximation">small-angle approximation</Term>:

$$
\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta \qquad (\theta\ \text{in radians, small})
$$

How small is small? At 10° ($0.1745$ rad), $\sin\theta$ is 0.5% below $\theta$ and $\tan\theta$ is 1% above. For the angles in astronomy, millionths of a radian, the approximation is essentially exact.

## Formulas

### Degrees and radians

From $360° = 2\pi\ \text{rad}$, divide both sides by 360 or by $2\pi$ to get the conversion factors:

$$
1° = \frac{\pi}{180}\ \text{rad} \approx 0.01745\ \text{rad}, \qquad 1\ \text{rad} = \frac{180°}{\pi} \approx 57.30°
$$

### Arc length

A full turn ($2\pi$ rad) sweeps the whole circumference, $2\pi r$. An angle $\theta$ is the fraction $\theta/(2\pi)$ of a full turn, so it sweeps that fraction of the circumference:

$$
s = \frac{\theta}{2\pi} \times 2\pi r = r\theta \qquad (\theta\ \text{in radians})
$$

This is why radians are natural. In degrees the same formula would be $s = \dfrac{\pi r \theta}{180}$.

### The Pythagorean identity

On the unit circle the point $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ is a distance 1 from the centre. By Pythagoras:

$$
\sin^{2}\theta + \cos^{2}\theta = 1
$$

(Here $\sin^{2}\theta$ means $(\sin\theta)^{2}$.) This holds for *every* angle.

### Tangent from sine and cosine

Divide the definitions: $\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \dfrac{\text{opp}/\text{hyp}}{\text{adj}/\text{hyp}} = \dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}$, so

$$
\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}.
$$

This also explains why $\tan 90°$ is undefined: $\cos 90° = 0$, and you can't divide by zero.

### Angular size of a distant object

An object of width $D$ seen from a distance $d$ much bigger than $D$ covers a thin slice of a circle of radius $d$. Its width is (very nearly) an arc, so by $s = r\theta$:

$$
\theta \approx \frac{D}{d} \qquad (\theta\ \text{in radians}, D \ll d)
$$

### Parallax distance

In the <Term id="parallax">parallax</Term> diagram below, the Sun, Earth (in July) and the star form a right-angled triangle. The side opposite the parallax angle $p$ is 1 au, and the side adjacent to it is the star's distance $d$. So $\tan p = \dfrac{1\ \text{au}}{d}$, which gives

$$
d = \frac{1\ \text{au}}{\tan p} \approx \frac{1\ \text{au}}{p} \qquad (p\ \text{in radians, tiny})
$$

## Worked examples

<Example n={1} title="A ladder against a wall">
A $5.0$ m ladder leans against a wall at $70°$ to the ground. How high up the wall does it reach, and how far is its foot from the wall?

The ladder is the hypotenuse. The height is opposite the 70° angle, and the distance along the ground is adjacent to it.

$$
\text{height} = 5.0\ \text{m} \times \sin 70° = 5.0\ \text{m} \times 0.9397 = 4.7\ \text{m}
$$

$$
\text{foot} = 5.0\ \text{m} \times \cos 70° = 5.0\ \text{m} \times 0.3420 = 1.7\ \text{m}
$$

**Check with Pythagoras:** $4.698^{2} + 1.710^{2} = 22.07 + 2.92 = 24.99 \approx 25.0 = 5.0^{2}$ ✓.
</Example>

<Example n={2} title="How big does the Sun look?">
The Sun's diameter is $1.391 \times 10^{9}$ m and it is $1.496 \times 10^{11}$ m away. What angle does it span in the sky, in radians and in degrees?

The Sun is tiny compared with its distance, so use $\theta \approx D/d$:

$$
\theta = \frac{1.391 \times 10^{9}\ \text{m}}{1.496 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}} = 9.30 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{rad}
$$

$$
\theta = 9.30 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{rad} \times \frac{180°}{\pi\ \text{rad}} = 0.533°
$$

About half a degree, the width of your little fingernail held at arm's length. NASA lists the Sun's apparent diameter as 1919 arcseconds, which is $1919/3600 = 0.533°$ ✓.
</Example>

<Example n={3} title="Measuring the distance to Tau Ceti" book>
Tau Ceti's measured parallax is $p = 273.81$ milliarcseconds. Find its distance in au, metres and light-years.

**Step 1: convert the angle to radians.** An arcsecond is $\tfrac{1}{3600}$ of a degree, and a milliarcsecond is a thousandth of that:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= 273.81 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{arcsec} \times \frac{1°}{3600\ \text{arcsec}} \times \frac{\pi\ \text{rad}}{180°} \\
  &= 1.3275 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{rad}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2: use the small-angle form** (at an angle this tiny, $\tan p$ and $p$ agree to about a millionth of a millionth):

$$
d = \frac{1\ \text{au}}{p} = \frac{1\ \text{au}}{1.3275 \times 10^{-6}} = 7.533 \times 10^{5}\ \text{au}
$$

**Step 3: convert.**

$$
d = 7.533 \times 10^{5} \times 1.496 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m} = 1.127 \times 10^{17}\ \text{m}
$$

$$
d = \frac{1.127 \times 10^{17}\ \text{m}}{9.461 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m/ly}} = 11.91\ \text{ly}
$$

This is where the novel's "about twelve light-years" comes from. The parallax angle is about the width of a 2 cm coin seen from 15 km away.
</Example>

<Example n={4} title="The Sun seen from Tau Ceti" book>
When Grace reaches Tau Ceti, how big does the Sun look from there, in milliarcseconds?

$$
\theta = \frac{D}{d} = \frac{1.391 \times 10^{9}\ \text{m}}{1.127 \times 10^{17}\ \text{m}} = 1.234 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{rad}
$$

Convert, using $1\ \text{rad} = 57.30°$ and $1° = 3.6 \times 10^{6}$ mas:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta &= 1.234 \times 10^{-8} \times 57.30 \times 3.6 \times 10^{6}\ \text{mas} \\
       &= 2.55\ \text{mas}
\end{aligned}
$$

The human eye can resolve about 60 arcseconds, which is 60 000 mas, so the Sun is a pure point of light from there, like any other star.
</Example>

<Example n={5} title="How far does Earth travel in one day?">
Earth goes once round its (nearly circular) orbit of radius $1\ \text{au} = 1.496 \times 10^{11}$ m in 365.25 days. How far does it travel in one day, and what speed is that?

In one day Earth turns through

$$
\theta = \frac{2\pi\ \text{rad}}{365.25} = 0.017\,20\ \text{rad}.
$$

Arc length:

$$
s = r\theta = 1.496 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m} \times 0.017\,20 = 2.574 \times 10^{9}\ \text{m}
$$

Speed is distance over time:

$$
v = \frac{2.574 \times 10^{9}\ \text{m}}{86\,400\ \text{s}} = 2.979 \times 10^{4}\ \text{m/s} = 29.79\ \text{km/s}
$$

This matches NASA's 29.78 km/s, which we used in the last chapter. Radians made it a one-line calculation.
</Example>

## Real physics vs. book physics

<RealVsBook>
**Established science.** Trigonometric parallax is the foundation of all astronomical distance measurement. The Gaia space telescope measures parallaxes to a few hundredths of a milliarcsecond. Its value for Tau Ceti, $273.81 \pm 0.17$ mas, gives $11.912 \pm 0.007$ light-years. The Sun's apparent diameter of about 0.53° is standard.

**Weir's inventions.** None in this chapter.

**How realistic are the numbers?** The novel's distance to Tau Ceti is correct. A detail the book gets right implicitly: from Tau Ceti, our Sun would look like an ordinary, fairly bright star, not a disc. You'd need to know where to look to find home.
</RealVsBook>

## Practice set

<Problem n={1} level="easy">
Convert: (a) $90°$ and $270°$ to radians (exact, in terms of $\pi$) (b) $2.0$ rad to degrees (c) $0.5°$ to radians.

<Solution>
(a) $90° \times \dfrac{\pi}{180°} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ rad. $270° \times \dfrac{\pi}{180°} = \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$ rad.

(b) $2.0 \times \dfrac{180°}{\pi} = 114.6° \approx 115°$.

(c) $0.5 \times \dfrac{\pi}{180} = 8.73 \times 10^{-3}$ rad.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={2} level="easy">
A right-angled triangle has legs $3.0$ cm and $4.0$ cm. Find the hypotenuse, and the angle opposite the 3.0 cm side.

<Solution>
$c = \sqrt{3.0^{2} + 4.0^{2}} = \sqrt{25} = 5.0$ cm.

The 3.0 cm side is opposite the angle and the 4.0 cm side is adjacent, so $\tan\theta = 3.0/4.0 = 0.75$, giving $\theta = \tan^{-1}(0.75) = 36.9°$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={3} level="easy">
A ramp rises $1.2$ m over a horizontal distance of $4.0$ m. Find its angle to the horizontal and its sloping length.

<Solution>
$\tan\theta = \dfrac{1.2}{4.0} = 0.30$, so $\theta = \tan^{-1}(0.30) = 16.7°$.

Length $= \sqrt{1.2^{2} + 4.0^{2}} = \sqrt{17.44} = 4.18$ m.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={4} level="easy">
Without a calculator, give $\sin 150°$, $\cos 150°$ and $\sin 270°$ using the unit circle.

<Solution>
$150°$ is in the second quarter, $30°$ back from $180°$. The point is the mirror image of the $30°$ point across the vertical axis: $(-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$. So $\sin 150° = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $\cos 150° = -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx -0.866$.

$270°$ points straight down to $(0, -1)$, so $\sin 270° = -1$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={5} level="medium">
A wheel of radius $0.35$ m rolls without slipping through an angle of $5.0$ rad. How far does it move?

<Solution>
The distance rolled equals the arc length of rim that touched the ground:

$$
s = r\theta = 0.35\ \text{m} \times 5.0 = 1.75\ \text{m} \approx 1.8\ \text{m}.
$$

(The radian is a ratio of two lengths, so it "disappears" from the units, leaving metres.)
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={6} level="medium">
The Moon is $3.844 \times 10^{8}$ m away and has a diameter of $3.475 \times 10^{6}$ m. Find its angular size in degrees, and compare it with the Sun's 0.533°.

<Solution>
$$
\theta = \frac{3.475 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}}{3.844 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}} = 9.04 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{rad} = 9.04 \times 10^{-3} \times 57.30° = 0.518°
$$

Almost the same as the Sun's 0.533°. That coincidence is why total solar eclipses are possible: the Moon can just cover the Sun's disc.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={7} level="medium">
Check the small-angle approximation at $\theta = 5.0°$. Find $\theta$ in radians, $\sin\theta$ and $\tan\theta$, and the percentage differences.

<Solution>
$\theta = 5.0 \times \pi/180 = 0.08727$ rad.

$\sin 5.0° = 0.08716$, which is $\dfrac{0.08716 - 0.08727}{0.08727} = -0.13\%$ from $\theta$.

$\tan 5.0° = 0.08749$, which is $+0.25\%$ from $\theta$.

Both are within a quarter of a percent, and the approximation gets better as the angle shrinks.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={8} level="medium" book>
A star has a parallax of $0.10$ arcseconds. How far away is it in au and in light-years ($1\ \text{ly} = 63\,240$ au)? Is it nearer or farther than Tau Ceti?

<Solution>
$p = 0.10\ \text{arcsec} \times \dfrac{1°}{3600\ \text{arcsec}} \times \dfrac{\pi}{180°} = 4.85 \times 10^{-7}$ rad.

$$
d = \frac{1\ \text{au}}{4.85 \times 10^{-7}} = 2.06 \times 10^{6}\ \text{au} = \frac{2.06 \times 10^{6}}{63\,240}\ \text{ly} = 32.6\ \text{ly}
$$

Farther than Tau Ceti. That makes sense: its parallax (100 mas) is smaller than Tau Ceti's (274 mas), and distance is inversely proportional to parallax.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={9} level="medium">
Use $\sin^{2}\theta + \cos^{2}\theta = 1$ to find $\cos\theta$ if $\sin\theta = 0.6$ and $\theta$ is between 90° and 180°.

<Solution>
$\cos^{2}\theta = 1 - 0.6^{2} = 1 - 0.36 = 0.64$, so $\cos\theta = \pm 0.8$. In the second quarter of the unit circle, $x$ is negative, so $\cos\theta = -0.8$. (The angle is $180° - 36.87° = 143.13°$.)
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={10} level="challenging" book>
The parallax shift is hard to measure because it's so small. If astronomers could measure from a baseline of 1 light-year instead of 1 au (say with a probe sent far out), what parallax angle would Tau Ceti show, in degrees?

<Solution>
Now the side opposite $p$ is 1 ly and the adjacent side is 11.91 ly:

$$
\tan p = \frac{1\ \text{ly}}{11.91\ \text{ly}} = 0.0840 \quad\Rightarrow\quad p = \tan^{-1}(0.0840) = 4.80°.
$$

This angle isn't small, so we use $\tan^{-1}$ rather than the small-angle shortcut. (The shortcut gives $0.0840$ rad $= 4.81°$, still only 0.2% off.) A bigger baseline makes a much bigger, easier angle. Interstellar travellers like Grace could measure stellar distances easily.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={11} level="challenging">
A satellite dish must point at a satellite that is $35\,786$ km above the equator. From a point on the equator directly below it, how far away is the satellite? From a point on the equator $40°$ of longitude away, how far is it? (Earth's radius is $6378$ km. Hint: draw the triangle from Earth's centre.)

<Solution>
Directly below: the distance is just the altitude, $35\,786$ km.

For the second point, draw the triangle Earth's centre (O), observer (A), satellite (S). OA $= 6378$ km, OS $= 6378 + 35\,786 = 42\,164$ km, and the angle at O is $40°$. This isn't a right-angled triangle, so split OS using the observer's position. The perpendicular from A onto OS has length $6378 \sin 40° = 4099.7$ km, and its foot is $6378\cos 40° = 4885.9$ km from O. That leaves $42\,164 - 4885.9 = 37\,278.1$ km between the foot and S. By Pythagoras:

$$
\text{AS} = \sqrt{4099.7^{2} + 37\,278.1^{2}}\ \text{km} = \sqrt{1.6808 \times 10^{7} + 1.38966 \times 10^{9}}\ \text{km} = 37\,503\ \text{km}.
$$

About 37 500 km, only 5% farther than from directly below.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={12} level="challenging">
Show that for small $\theta$ (in radians), $\cos\theta \approx 1 - \dfrac{\theta^{2}}{2}$. Use $\sin^{2}\theta + \cos^{2}\theta = 1$, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, and the fact that $\sqrt{1 - x} \approx 1 - \tfrac{x}{2}$ for small $x$ (check that last one with $x = 0.01$). Then test it at $\theta = 0.2$ rad.

<Solution>
First check the hint: $\sqrt{1 - 0.01} = \sqrt{0.99} = 0.994\,99$, and $1 - 0.005 = 0.995$ ✓.

From the identity, $\cos\theta = \sqrt{1 - \sin^{2}\theta}$ (positive for small angles). With $\sin\theta \approx \theta$:

$$
\cos\theta \approx \sqrt{1 - \theta^{2}} \approx 1 - \frac{\theta^{2}}{2}.
$$

Test at $\theta = 0.2$: the formula gives $1 - 0.04/2 = 0.98$. The true value is $\cos 0.2 = 0.980\,07$. They agree to better than 0.01%. Part 2 uses this result in the pendulum.
</Solution>
</Problem>

## Summary

- A full turn is $360° = 2\pi$ rad. One radian is the angle whose arc equals the radius, about 57.3°.
- In a right-angled triangle: $\sin\theta = \text{opp}/\text{hyp}$, $\cos\theta = \text{adj}/\text{hyp}$, $\tan\theta = \text{opp}/\text{adj}$. Inverse functions turn a ratio back into an angle.
- Pythagoras: $a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}$. On the unit circle, the point at angle $\theta$ is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, which extends sine and cosine to any angle.
- Arc length $s = r\theta$ and angular size $\theta \approx D/d$, both with $\theta$ in radians.
- For small angles in radians, $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta$. Parallax distance is $d \approx 1\ \text{au}/p$.

**Formulas from this chapter**

$$
\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}} \quad \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}} \quad \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}} = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} \qquad a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2} \qquad \sin^{2}\theta + \cos^{2}\theta = 1
$$

$$
180° = \pi\ \text{rad} \qquad s = r\theta \qquad \theta \approx \frac{D}{d} \qquad \sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta \qquad d \approx \frac{1\ \text{au}}{p}
$$

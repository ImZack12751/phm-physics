## The scene

Astrophage does something no living thing on Earth can: it stores energy as **mass**. When it soaks up more energy than it needs to stay at 96.415 °C, the extra becomes mass; when it needs energy, it turns mass back into light. In the novel, Dimitri's first test destroys about 20 micrograms of Astrophage and releases enough light to push his apparatus with 60 000 newtons. And the *Hail Mary*'s drive burns about six grams a second for 540 trillion watts.

Since Chapter 5.3 this course has used Einstein's $E = mc^{2}$ to connect those numbers, and promised to explain it. With the momentum of light (Chapter 7.4) and the centre of mass (Chapter 3.3) we can now derive it, in a few lines, with no relativity needed.

## The concept

### A puzzle about a box

Take a closed box floating in space, far from everything. A lamp on its left wall sends a flash of light to its right wall, where the flash is absorbed. Nothing outside touched the box. So, by conservation of momentum (Chapter 5.5), its **centre of mass** (Chapter 3.3) can't move.

But light carries momentum, $E/c$ (Chapter 7.4). When the flash leaves the left wall, the box recoils to the left, like a gun firing. While the flash crosses, the box drifts left. When the flash hits the right wall, it hands its momentum over and the box stops. The box has **moved to the left**, and nothing else seems to have moved. The centre of mass has shifted, which is impossible.

### The way out: light carries mass

The only escape is that the flash carried some mass from the left wall to the right wall. Then the box shifting left while a little mass moves right leaves the centre of mass exactly where it was. Working through the numbers (Formulas section) gives the mass the flash must carry:

$$
m = \frac{E}{c^{2}}.
$$

The size of the box, its mass and the length all cancel out. Any energy $E$, moved from one place to another, carries mass $E/c^{2}$ with it. Turn it around: a mass $m$ is a store of energy

$$
E = mc^{2}.
$$

Einstein first reached the result in 1905, in a paper asking "Does the inertia of a body depend upon its energy content?", and in 1906 he gave a centre-of-mass argument much like this one. The answer to his question is yes: energy has inertia. This is the principle of <Term id="mass-energy-equivalence">mass–energy equivalence</Term>.

### Mass is a form of energy

$E = mc^{2}$ says more than "you can turn mass into energy". It says mass **is** energy, counted in different units. Every form of energy a body contains adds to its mass: heat it and it gets heavier; wind up a spring inside it and it gets heavier; charge a battery and it gets heavier. The energy a body has simply by existing, at rest, is its <Term id="rest-energy">rest energy</Term> $mc^{2}$. (Part 10 adds what happens when it moves fast.)

### Why nobody noticed: c² is enormous

$c^{2} = (2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s})^{2} = 8.988 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J/kg}$. That is the energy in each kilogram: about ninety thousand trillion joules, as much as the whole world uses in an hour and a quarter (Example 1).

So everyday energy changes make almost no difference to mass. Heating a cup of coffee adds less than a nanogram. Burning fuel releases only about a billionth of the fuel's rest energy: the products weigh less than the fuel and oxygen did, but by far too little to weigh. The rule "mass is conserved" in chemistry is an excellent approximation precisely because $c^{2}$ is so large.

### Where the mass really changes: nuclei

Inside atomic nuclei the forces are millions of times stronger than in chemistry, and the mass changes become measurable. Weigh a helium-4 atom and it comes out **lighter** than the four hydrogen atoms that the Sun fuses to make it, by 0.7%. That missing mass, the <Term id="mass-defect">mass defect</Term>, left as energy.

This is what powers the Sun. Its luminosity, $3.828 \times 10^{26}$ W, means it loses $L/c^{2} = 4.26 \times 10^{9}$ kg of mass every second: over four million tonnes. Splitting uranium (fission) releases about 0.09% of the fuel's mass; fusing deuterium and tritium about 0.4%.

### All of it: antimatter

Every particle has an antiparticle with the same mass and opposite charge. When an electron meets its antiparticle, the positron, both vanish and their entire rest energy appears as two gamma-ray photons of 511 keV each. Hospitals use exactly this in PET scanners. Matter meeting antimatter is the only known way to turn **all** of a mass into energy.

### Astrophage obeys the rule

The novel's Astrophage is, in effect, a perfect mass–energy converter: it turns light into its own mass and back again. Its invention is the mechanism, not the bookkeeping. All of the novel's numbers obey $E = mc^{2}$: Dimitri's 20 µg makes $1.8 \times 10^{9}$ J, and the drive's 6.043 g/s makes 543 TW (Example 2). In the novel, the energy is stored as neutrinos inside the cell, which is fiction, but by $E = mc^{2}$ any stored energy, however it is held, would add exactly $E/c^{2}$ to the cell's mass.

## Formulas

### Light in a box

A box of mass $M$ and length $L$ sends a flash of energy $E$ from its left wall to its right wall.

**Recoil.** The flash carries momentum $E/c$ to the right, so by conservation of momentum the box moves left with

$$
Mv = \frac{E}{c} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad v = \frac{E}{Mc}
$$

(the box moves so slowly that $L$ barely changes, and its shift is tiny compared with $L$).

**Drift.** The flash takes $t = L/c$ to cross, so the box shifts left by

$$
\Delta x = vt = \frac{E}{Mc}\cdot\frac{L}{c} = \frac{EL}{Mc^{2}}.
$$

**Centre of mass.** With no outside force, the centre of mass stays put (Chapter 3.3: $m_{1}r_{1} = m_{2}r_{2}$ about it). If the flash carried a mass $m$ a distance $L$ to the right while the box's mass $M$ moved $\Delta x$ to the left, then

$$
mL = M\,\Delta x = M\cdot\frac{EL}{Mc^{2}} = \frac{EL}{c^{2}} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \boxed{m = \frac{E}{c^{2}}} \qquad \boxed{E = mc^{2}}
$$

### Energy and mass changes

Any change in a body's energy changes its mass:

$$
\boxed{\Delta m = \frac{\Delta E}{c^{2}}} \qquad c^{2} = 8.988 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J/kg}
$$

A steady power $P$ means a steady mass change $\dot{m} = P/c^{2}$ (Chapter 5.3's $P = \dot{m}c^{2}$ the other way round).

### Mass defect

For a nuclear reaction, add up the masses before and after; the difference, times $c^{2}$, is the energy released. In atomic mass units, $1\ \text{u} \times c^{2} = 931.5$ MeV:

$$
\boxed{Q = (m_{\text{before}} - m_{\text{after}})\,c^{2}} \qquad E_{\text{MeV}} = 931.5 \times \Delta m_{\text{u}}
$$

## Worked examples

<Example n={1} title="The energy in a kilogram">
Find the rest energy of 1 kg and of 1 g. The world uses about 19.6 TW on average. How long would 1 kg run the world? What mass of fuel, fully converted, would run it for a year?

$$
E = mc^{2} = 1\ \text{kg} \times (2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s})^{2} = 8.988 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J} \qquad 1\ \text{g}: 8.988 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}
$$

$$
t = \frac{8.988 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J}}{1.96 \times 10^{13}\ \text{W}} = 4590\ \text{s} \approx 1.3\ \text{hours}
$$

$$
E_{\text{year}} = 1.96 \times 10^{13}\ \text{W} \times 3.156 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s} = 6.19 \times 10^{20}\ \text{J} \qquad m = \frac{6.19 \times 10^{20}\ \text{J}}{8.988 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J/kg}} = 6880\ \text{kg}
$$

About seven tonnes of matter, completely converted, would power human civilisation for a year.
</Example>

<Example n={2} title="Checking the novel's numbers" book>
Dimitri destroys about 20 µg of Astrophage, and the drive burns 6.043 g/s. Find the energy of the first and the power of the second.

$$
E = mc^{2} = 20 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{kg} \times 8.988 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J/kg} = 1.80 \times 10^{9}\ \text{J}
$$

$$
P = \dot{m}c^{2} = 6.043 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{kg/s} \times 8.988 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J/kg} = 5.43 \times 10^{14}\ \text{W}
$$

The novel's $1.8 \times 10^{9}$ J and 540 TW are exactly what $E = mc^{2}$ demands (Grace's 540 TW used 6 g/s and $c = 3 \times 10^{8}$ m/s). Twenty micrograms, a speck you could barely see, holds as much energy as about 430 kg of TNT.
</Example>

<Example n={3} title="The Sun is getting lighter">
The Sun's luminosity is $3.828 \times 10^{26}$ W. How much mass does it lose each second as light? Over its 4.6 billion years, what fraction of its $1.988 \times 10^{30}$ kg is that?

$$
\dot{m} = \frac{L}{c^{2}} = \frac{3.828 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}}{8.988 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J/kg}} = 4.26 \times 10^{9}\ \text{kg/s}
$$

$$
\Delta m = 4.26 \times 10^{9}\ \text{kg/s} \times 4.6 \times 10^{9} \times 3.156 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s} = 6.2 \times 10^{26}\ \text{kg} \qquad \frac{6.2 \times 10^{26}}{1.988 \times 10^{30}} = 0.031\%
$$

Four million tonnes a second, about a hundred times the Earth's mass over the Sun's lifetime so far, yet only three hundredths of a percent of the Sun.
</Example>

<Example n={4} title="The Sun's fuel">
Four hydrogen atoms (1.007 825 u each) fuse, in steps, into one helium-4 atom (4.002 603 u). Find the mass defect, the fraction of the mass released and the energy per helium atom. How much hydrogen does the Sun fuse each second?

$$
\Delta m = 4 \times 1.007\,825\ \text{u} - 4.002\,603\ \text{u} = 4.031\,300\ \text{u} - 4.002\,603\ \text{u} = 0.028\,697\ \text{u}
$$

$$
\frac{\Delta m}{m} = \frac{0.028\,697}{4.031\,300} = 0.712\% \qquad E = 0.028\,697 \times 931.5\ \text{MeV} = 26.7\ \text{MeV}
$$

$$
\dot{m}_{\text{H}} = \frac{4.26 \times 10^{9}\ \text{kg/s}}{0.00712} = 6.0 \times 10^{11}\ \text{kg/s}
$$

The Sun turns about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium every second, and 0.7% of it vanishes as light.
</Example>

<Example n={5} title="Hot coffee weighs more">
A 250 g cup of coffee (treat it as water, $c = 4186$ J/(kg·K)) is heated by 60 K. How much heavier is it?

$$
Q = mc\,\Delta T = 0.250\ \text{kg} \times 4186\ \text{J/(kg·K)} \times 60\ \text{K} = 6.28 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}
$$

$$
\Delta m = \frac{Q}{c^{2}} = \frac{6.28 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}}{8.988 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J/kg}} = 7.0 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{kg}
$$

0.7 nanograms, about three parts in a trillion of the coffee's mass. Real, but far beyond any kitchen scale (and Chapter 6.2's $c$ for specific heat is not the speed of light, despite the shared letter).
</Example>

## Real physics vs. book physics

<RealVsBook>
**Established science.** $E = mc^{2}$ (Einstein, 1905, with a centre-of-mass argument in 1906) is one of the best-tested laws in physics. Atomic masses (NIST) give the 0.712% mass defect of fusing hydrogen into helium; the Sun's mass loss follows from its luminosity; electron–positron annihilation into 511 keV photons is used in PET scanners; and heated or compressed objects really do gain mass, though far too little to weigh in daily life.

**Weir's inventions.** Astrophage converting light into mass and back, at will, is fiction, and so is its neutrino storage. No known process does it. But the novel's numbers for Dimitri's test and the drive obey $E = mc^{2}$ exactly.

**How realistic are the numbers?** Completely, given the premise. A 100% mass–energy converter would be the most powerful fuel physics allows, which is why the novel's choice of Astrophage as a fuel makes a starship possible at all. Chapter 9.2 compares it with every real fuel.
</RealVsBook>

An electron and a positron at rest annihilate into two photons of equal energy. What is each photon's energy and wavelength? (Use $E_{\text{eV}} = 1240/\lambda_{\text{nm}}$.)

## Summary

- Light carries momentum $E/c$, so a flash crossing a closed box makes the box shift. The centre of mass can't move, so the flash must carry mass $E/c^{2}$: $E = mc^{2}$.
- Mass is a form of energy. Every energy a body holds adds $E/c^{2}$ to its mass; its rest energy is $mc^{2}$.
- $c^{2} = 8.988 \times 10^{16}$ J/kg is huge, so chemical and thermal energy changes alter mass by parts in a billion or less.
- In nuclear reactions the mass defect is measurable: hydrogen fusing to helium loses 0.712%, 26.7 MeV per helium atom. The Sun loses $4.26 \times 10^{9}$ kg/s.
- Matter–antimatter annihilation converts all of the mass. The novel's Astrophage is a perfect converter, and all its numbers obey $E = mc^{2}$.

**Formulas from this chapter**

$$
E = mc^{2} \qquad \Delta m = \frac{\Delta E}{c^{2}} \qquad P = \dot{m}c^{2} \qquad Q = (m_{\text{before}} - m_{\text{after}})c^{2},\ \ 1\ \text{u}\,c^{2} = 931.5\ \text{MeV}
$$

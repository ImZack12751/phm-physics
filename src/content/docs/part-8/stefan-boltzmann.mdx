## The scene

Before the *Hail Mary* is even designed, astronomers have to answer a simple-sounding question: how much is the Sun's output really falling? The Sun puts out an almost unimaginable power, and the forecast says it will drop by 1% in about nine years and 5% in about twenty. What does a star's total power depend on, and what would it take to change it?

Chapter 6.3 introduced the law that answers this, $P = e\sigma AT^{4}$, as a measured fact and promised to explain where it comes from. Now that we have Planck's law (Chapter 8.1), we can.

## The concept

### Adding up the whole spectrum

Planck's law tells us how much a blackbody emits at each wavelength. The **total** power from each square metre is everything added up: the area under the Planck curve (Chapter C.4). Hotter objects glow more at every wavelength, so the area grows with temperature. But how fast?

### Why it has to be T⁴

Chapter 8.1 showed that Planck's law can be written in terms of $x = hc/\lambda kT$ as

$$
B_{\lambda} = \frac{2(kT)^{5}}{h^{4}c^{3}}\cdot\frac{x^{5}}{e^{x} - 1}.
$$

That means the curve always has the **same shape**, just resized. Raise the temperature by some factor, say 1.5, and two things happen to the curve:

- every feature moves to a wavelength 1.5 times **shorter** (the curve is squeezed sideways by 1.5, as Wien's law says);
- every height is multiplied by $1.5^{5} = 7.6$ (from the $(kT)^{5}$ in front).

An area is width times height, so the total grows by $7.6/1.5 = 1.5^{4} = 5.1$. Whatever the factor, the total grows as its fourth power.

So the power from each square metre of a blackbody is

$$
\frac{P}{A} = \sigma T^{4}.
$$

This is the <Term id="stefan-boltzmann-law">Stefan–Boltzmann law</Term>. Josef Stefan found the $T^{4}$ rule from measurements in 1879, and Ludwig Boltzmann explained it with theory in 1884. Working out the area exactly (a harder integral, done in the Formulas section) gives the constant in terms of nature's basic numbers:

$$
\sigma = \frac{2\pi^{5}k^{4}}{15c^{2}h^{3}} = 5.670 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W/(m}^{2}\text{K}^{4}).
$$

The constant Chapter 6.3 took from measurement is built from the Boltzmann constant, the speed of light and the Planck constant. Stefan himself used his law to make the first sensible estimate of the Sun's surface temperature, about 5700 K.

### The fourth power is fierce

$T^{4}$ means small temperature changes make big differences. Double the temperature and the glow is 16 times stronger; a surface at 1000 K radiates about 57 kW from each square metre, while the Sun's surface, at 5772 K, pours out 63 million watts per square metre. Run it the other way: a 1% change in output needs only a ¼% change in temperature.

### Luminosity: the whole star

A star's total power, its <Term id="luminosity">luminosity</Term>, is its surface area times what each square metre emits. A sphere of radius $R$ has area $4\pi R^{2}$, so

$$
L = 4\pi R^{2}\sigma T^{4}.
$$

Here $T$ is the star's **effective temperature**: the temperature of a blackbody of the same size that would give out the same power. (A star isn't a perfect blackbody, but it's close.) Two things set how bright a star is: its size, through $R^{2}$, and its temperature, through $T^{4}$. A huge cool star can outshine a small hot one.

Compared with the Sun this is easiest as a ratio (Chapter 0.5):

$$
\frac{L}{L_{\odot}} = \left(\frac{R}{R_{\odot}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{T}{T_{\odot}}\right)^{4}.
$$

### Measuring the sizes of stars

Stars are so far away that they look like points, even in big telescopes. But astronomers can measure a star's **temperature** from its colour or spectrum (Chapters 8.1 and 7.5), and its **luminosity** from how bright it looks and how far away it is (Chapter 8.3). Then the Stefan–Boltzmann law gives its radius. That is how we know that Sirius A is 1.7 times the Sun's size and Proxima Centauri only about a seventh.

### What a dimming would mean

The novel's crisis is a Sun putting out less light. If the Sun itself were cooling, a 1% drop in $L$ would need its surface to cool by only about 14 K, a quarter of a percent, too little to notice in its colour. But that is not what happens in the novel. The Sun's output is being **intercepted**: Astrophage soaks up sunlight and turns the energy into its own mass. A 1% loss of the Sun's light is 1% of $3.828 \times 10^{26}$ W, almost four trillion trillion watts, being eaten.

## Formulas

### The Stefan–Boltzmann law

The power emitted per square metre per metre of wavelength is $\pi B_{\lambda}$, so the total is

$$
\frac{P}{A} = \int_{0}^{\infty} \pi B_{\lambda}\,d\lambda.
$$

Change variable to $x = hc/\lambda kT$, so $\lambda = hc/(xkT)$ and a small step $d\lambda$ corresponds to $\dfrac{hc}{x^{2}kT}\,dx$ (the size of the step; the limits swap round, which absorbs the minus sign):

$$
\frac{P}{A} = \pi \cdot \frac{2(kT)^{5}}{h^{4}c^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{x^{5}}{e^{x} - 1}\cdot\frac{hc}{x^{2}kT}\,dx = \frac{2\pi k^{4}T^{4}}{h^{3}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{3}}{e^{x} - 1}\,dx
$$

The temperature has come out in front as $T^{4}$, exactly as the squeeze-and-stretch argument said. The remaining integral is a pure number; it can be evaluated (with techniques beyond this course) as $\pi^{4}/15 = 6.494$. So

$$
\boxed{\frac{P}{A} = \sigma T^{4}} \qquad \sigma = \frac{2\pi k^{4}}{h^{3}c^{2}}\cdot\frac{\pi^{4}}{15} = \frac{2\pi^{5}k^{4}}{15c^{2}h^{3}} = 5.670 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W/(m}^{2}\text{K}^{4})
$$

For a real surface with emissivity $e$, multiply by $e$ (Chapter 6.3).

### Luminosity

$$
\boxed{L = 4\pi R^{2}\sigma T^{4}} \qquad \boxed{\frac{L}{L_{\odot}} = \left(\frac{R}{R_{\odot}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{T}{T_{\odot}}\right)^{4}}
$$

Rearranged for the radius or the temperature:

$$
\frac{R}{R_{\odot}} = \frac{\sqrt{L/L_{\odot}}}{(T/T_{\odot})^{2}} \qquad T = \left(\frac{L}{4\pi R^{2}\sigma}\right)^{1/4}
$$

### Small changes

Differentiate $L = 4\pi R^{2}\sigma T^{4}$ with respect to $T$ at fixed $R$: $\dfrac{dL}{dT} = 4 \cdot 4\pi R^{2}\sigma T^{3} = \dfrac{4L}{T}$. For a small change,

$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta L}{L} \approx 4\,\frac{\Delta T}{T}} \qquad \frac{\Delta T}{T} \approx \frac{1}{4}\,\frac{\Delta L}{L}
$$

## Worked examples

<Example n={1} title="The Sun's power from its size and temperature">
The Sun's radius is $6.957 \times 10^{8}$ m and its effective temperature 5772 K. Find the power from each square metre and its luminosity.

$$
\frac{P}{A} = \sigma T^{4} = 5.670 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W/(m}^{2}\text{K}^{4}) \times (5772\ \text{K})^{4} = 6.294 \times 10^{7}\ \text{W/m}^{2}
$$

$$
L = 4\pi R^{2}\,\sigma T^{4} = 4\pi (6.957 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m})^{2} \times 6.294 \times 10^{7}\ \text{W/m}^{2} = 3.828 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}
$$

Exactly the measured value. Each square metre of the Sun's surface shines with the power of about sixty thousand one-kilowatt heaters.
</Example>

<Example n={2} title="Sirius A's size">
Sirius A has a luminosity of 24.74 Suns and a surface temperature of 9845 K. How big is it?

$$
\frac{R}{R_{\odot}} = \frac{\sqrt{L/L_{\odot}}}{(T/T_{\odot})^{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{24.74}}{(9845/5772)^{2}} = \frac{4.974}{2.909} = 1.71
$$

Sirius A is 1.71 times the Sun's radius, matching the measured 1.714. Most of its extra brightness comes from its temperature: $(9845/5772)^{4} = 8.5$, compared with $1.71^{2} = 2.9$ from its size.
</Example>

<Example n={3} title="Cooling the Sun by 1%" book>
If the Sun's 1% dimming came from its surface cooling, by how much would the temperature have to fall? Compare the exact answer with the small-change rule.

$$
T_{\text{new}} = T\,(0.99)^{1/4} = 5772\ \text{K} \times 0.997\,490 = 5757.5\ \text{K} \qquad \Delta T = 14.5\ \text{K}
$$

$$
\text{small-change rule: } \Delta T \approx \frac{T}{4}\,\frac{\Delta L}{L} = \frac{5772\ \text{K}}{4} \times 0.01 = 14.4\ \text{K}
$$

A drop of about 14 K, a quarter of a percent. The Sun's colour would hardly change. Astronomers would see the dimming in the total light long before they noticed any change in colour.
</Example>

<Example n={4} title="How much power does Astrophage eat?" book>
In the novel, Astrophage soaks up sunlight and turns the energy into mass. If it intercepts 1% of the Sun's output, how much power is that, and how much mass would it gain each second and each year if all the energy became mass ($E = mc^{2}$, Chapter 5.3)?

$$
P = 0.01 \times 3.828 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W} = 3.828 \times 10^{24}\ \text{W}
$$

$$
\frac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = \frac{P}{c^{2}} = \frac{3.828 \times 10^{24}\ \text{W}}{(2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s})^{2}} = 4.26 \times 10^{7}\ \text{kg/s}
$$

$$
\text{per year: } 4.26 \times 10^{7}\ \text{kg/s} \times 3.156 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s} = 1.34 \times 10^{15}\ \text{kg}
$$

Forty thousand tonnes of new Astrophage every second, more than a trillion tonnes a year, and the population keeps growing. That is the scale of the crisis.
</Example>

<Example n={5} title="The drive's waste heat" book>
The *Hail Mary*'s drive puts out about $5.4 \times 10^{14}$ W. Suppose just 0.1% of that ended up as heat in the ship (our assumption, for illustration). How large a radiator, glowing as a blackbody at 1000 K, would it need to get rid of that heat?

$$
P = 0.001 \times 5.4 \times 10^{14}\ \text{W} = 5.4 \times 10^{11}\ \text{W}
$$

$$
A = \frac{P}{\sigma T^{4}} = \frac{5.4 \times 10^{11}\ \text{W}}{5.670 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W/(m}^{2}\text{K}^{4}) \times (1000\ \text{K})^{4}} = 9.5 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}^{2}
$$

About 9.5 square kilometres of red-hot radiator, a square three kilometres on a side. In space, radiation is the only way to shed heat (Chapter 6.3), so the drive must waste almost nothing: the light has to leave the ship without heating it.
</Example>

## Real physics vs. book physics

<RealVsBook>
**Established science.** The Stefan–Boltzmann law (Stefan 1879, Boltzmann 1884), $\sigma = 2\pi^{5}k^{4}/(15c^{2}h^{3})$ (NIST), $L = 4\pi R^{2}\sigma T^{4}$, and the measured luminosities, radii and temperatures of the Sun, Sirius A and Proxima Centauri are standard astrophysics. Stefan really did estimate the Sun's temperature at about 5700 K. Stars' sizes really are found this way, and confirmed by direct measurements with interferometers.

**Weir's inventions.** Astrophage absorbing a percent of the Sun's light and turning it into mass is fiction. The 0.1% waste heat in Example 5 is our assumption; the novel doesn't give one.

**How realistic are the numbers?** A dimming of the Sun by a few percent through cooling would need only tens of kelvins, so a real dimming would be easy to measure in total light but hard to see in colour. The waste-heat estimate shows why a real light drive is so hard: at hundreds of terawatts, even tiny inefficiencies would melt the ship. The novel's drive must be extraordinarily efficient, and the book treats it that way.
</RealVsBook>

<Problem n={7} level="medium">

Compute $\sigma$ from $\sigma = 2\pi^{5}k^{4}/(15c^{2}h^{3})$ with $k = 1.380\,649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, $c = 2.998 \times 10^{8}$ m/s and $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s.

$2\pi^{5} = 612.0$; $k^{4} = 3.634 \times 10^{-92}$; $15c^{2}h^{3} = 15 \times 8.988 \times 10^{16} \times 2.909 \times 10^{-100} = 3.922 \times 10^{-82}$. So $\sigma = 612.0 \times 3.634 \times 10^{-92}/3.922 \times 10^{-82} = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}$ W/(m²K⁴).

Use the small-change rule to find how much the Sun's surface would have to cool for the novel's 5% dimming. Check with the exact formula.

## Summary

- The total power per square metre of a blackbody is the area under Planck's curve. Raising $T$ squeezes the curve sideways by the same factor and stretches it upwards by its fifth power, so the area grows as $T^{4}$: $P/A = \sigma T^{4}$.
- $\sigma = 2\pi^{5}k^{4}/(15c^{2}h^{3}) = 5.670 \times 10^{-8}$ W/(m²K⁴), built from $k$, $c$ and $h$.
- A star's luminosity is $L = 4\pi R^{2}\sigma T^{4}$; relative to the Sun, $L/L_{\odot} = (R/R_{\odot})^{2}(T/T_{\odot})^{4}$. Luminosity and temperature give a star's radius.
- Small changes: $\Delta L/L \approx 4\,\Delta T/T$, so a 1% dimmer Sun would be only 14 K cooler.
- In the novel, a 1% dimming means Astrophage is eating about $3.8 \times 10^{24}$ W, gaining over $4 \times 10^{7}$ kg of mass per second.

**Formulas from this chapter**

$$
\frac{P}{A} = \sigma T^{4} \qquad \sigma = \frac{2\pi^{5}k^{4}}{15c^{2}h^{3}} \qquad L = 4\pi R^{2}\sigma T^{4} \qquad \frac{L}{L_{\odot}} = \left(\frac{R}{R_{\odot}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{T}{T_{\odot}}\right)^{4} \qquad \frac{\Delta L}{L} \approx 4\frac{\Delta T}{T}
$$
